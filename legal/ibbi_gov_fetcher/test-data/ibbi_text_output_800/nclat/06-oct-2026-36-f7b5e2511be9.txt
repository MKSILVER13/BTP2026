30.
ISSUE I- What is the nature and extent of the Adjudicating
Authority's power, under Section 33(1A) of the Code, over a restoration
application approved by the CoC with the requisite majority - is the CoC's
commercial decision immune from interference in the same manner as its
decision approving or rejecting a resolution plan under Sections 30 and 31
of the Code, or does the Adjudicating Authority retain an independent
discretion, and if so, of what character and subject to what constraints?
31.
We note that it is by now settled beyond controversy that the commercial
wisdom of the CoC, in matters entrusted to it under the scheme of the Code
principally, the approval or rejection of a resolution plan under Sections 30(4)
and 31, is not open to judicial review on merits, and that the jurisdiction of the
Adjudicating Authority, is confined to examining whether the decision-making
process prescribed by the Code has been observed. This position stands
authoritatively laid down in K. Sashidhar v. Indian Overseas Bank, (2019) 12
SCC 150; reaffirmed and elaborated in Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel
India Ltd. v. Satish Kumar Gupta, (2020) 8 SCC 531, holding that the
commercial wisdom of the CoC has been given paramountcy without any judicial
intervention, and that the Adjudicating Authority and the Appellate Tribunal
cannot substitute their views for the collective business decision of the financial
creditors.
32.
We take into consideration the fact that Section 33(1A), however, is not
framed in the same terms as Sections 30(2) and 31. Under the latter, once the

[PAGE 15]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1745 of 2026
Page 15 of 31

Adjudicating Authority is satisfied that a resolution plan approved by the CoC
meets the requirements of Section 30(2), it has no discretion to withhold approval;
its role is confined to a check on compliance. Section 33(1A), by contrast, on the
material before us, casts on the Adjudicating Authority a duty to consider the
CoC's application, and confers upon it a power, cast in terms of discretion ("may...
restore"), to allow or decline restoration, subject to the outer time-limit of 120
days. The provision does not, in terms, replicate the closed, compliance-only
architecture of Section 30(2)/31; nor, on the other hand, does it purport to hand
the Adjudicating Authority an unconfined power to reject a compliant application
on any ground it thinks fit.
33.
In our considered view, the correct position lies between these two poles.
Where the CoC's application satisfies the twin statutory conditions, namely, that
a ground under Section 33(1)(a) or (b) exists, and that the application carries the
approval of not less than 66% of the voting share, the residual discretion vested
in the Adjudicating Authority is a real one. However, the discretion vested must
accordingly be exercised judicially on relevant considerations in accordance to
the object of the provision i.e., whether a further time-bound attempt at resolution
is likely to serve, rather than defeat, the value-maximisation and resolution-first
objectives enshrined in the Code and not on considerations extraneous to the
record, or mere preference for a different commercial outcome than the one the
CoC, exercising its own commercial wisdom, has arrived at. This Appellate
Tribunal and the Adjudicating Authority ordinarily accord to the CoC's

[PAGE 16]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1745 of 2026
Page 16 of 31

commercial wisdom under Sections 30-31, even though it is not, the case of
complete ouster of the discretion of the Adjudicating Authority. In the other
words the considered CoC's decision, which is unanimous or comfortably
exceeds the statutory threshold, and is the request of deliberation across
successive meetings rather than a single meeting, then more cogent and specific
reasons must exist for declining the request of the CoC by the Adjudicating
Authority.
34.
Tested against this standard, we note that the Impugned Order itself
records, that the resolution for restoration was approved unanimously by 100%
voting share, comfortably exceeding the statutory threshold of 66%, and does not
anywhere suggest that the CoC's decision-making process suffered from any
procedural infirmity, or that it was tainted by mala fides or arrived at without due
deliberation. It is noted that indeed, the record (12th CoC Minutes) shows the
contrary as liquidation was commercial decision of the CoC member, against
liquidation and in favour of restoration. We find that in these circumstances, the
threshold conditions under Section 33(1A) stood not merely met but substantially
exceeded, and the burden was squarely on the Adjudicating Authority, if it were
to decline restoration after restoring specific and cogent, reasons capable of
displacing so considered and unanimous a commercial decision of the CoC.
Therefore, we find merit in the contentions of the Appellant on this issue, based
on the specific facts of the present case.

35.
ISSUE II- Whether the finding in the Impugned Order that
continuation of the CIRP would enable the Corporate Debtor's creditors to
"monetise unabsorbed losses...at the cost of the Government exchequer" is
sustainable, having regard to (a) whether it is founded on any material or
submission on the record; (b) whether it comports with the principles of
natural justice; and (c) whether, even taken at its highest, it is legally capable
of outweighing a unanimous, threshold-exceeding CoC decision?
36.
The Impugned Order records, that the Adjudicating Authority was
conscious that if CIRP is continued, the Creditors may be able to monetize
unabsorbed losses available to the Corporate Debtor at the cost of the
Government exchequer, and holds that the resolution of corporate debtor only
with objective of such monetization would not be in accordance with the spirit
and intent of IBC.
Based on the record before us, this consideration does not appear to have been
raised by any party or canvassed in the IA, or put to the Resolution Professional
or the CoC for an explanation before being relied upon by the Adjudicating
Authority before rejecting the application. We may infer that perhaps the only
reference to accumulated losses on this record is disclosure made by the
Resolution Professional in the ordinary course of his overview of the Corporate
Debtor's financial position at the 12th CoC meeting that the Corporate Debtor
carried forward losses of approximately Rs. 397 crores over the preceding four
years. It seems that such disclosure are made in insolvency proceedings and not

[PAGE 18]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1745 of 2026
Page 18 of 31

accompanied by, or connected to, any suggestion that such losses were intended
to be, or were capable of being, unlawfully monetised. We also note that the
findings in the Impugned order, does not identify the mechanism by which such
monetisation was apprehended to occur. The impugned order also does not
explain how it would operate at the cost of the Government exchequer, and does
not engage with the fact that the treatment of carried-forward business losses of
a corporate debtor is, in the first instance, governed by the provisions of the
Income-tax Act, 1961 and administered by the income-tax authorities, not by the
Adjudicating Authority under the Code. We are of opinion that the issue as to
whether, and to what extent, an incoming resolution applicant may set off or carry
forward such losses is a matter of substantive tax law and falls to be examined,
by the tax authorities in accordance with the relevant law. We also do not find
any specific and reasoned finding in the impugned order that the CoC's or the
RP's conduct involved fraud, or an abuse of the resolution process for an object
extraneous to the Code. It may be stated that the availability of such losses on the
Corporate Debtor's books is result of its financial history and cannot, be converted
into a disqualifying feature of a CoC approved restoration application without
any specific reasons. No such finding of fraud or abuse is recorded in the
Impugned Order.
37.
We also note that the finding is based on apprehension and possibility that
creditors may be able to monetise such losses, rather than as a definite finding.
We are of considered view that a conjectural apprehension of this nature,

[PAGE 19]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1745 of 2026
Page 19 of 31

unaccompanied by any supporting material, reasoning, or opportunity to the
affected party to respond, is an insufficient basis on which to override a
unanimous, commercial decision of the CoC, taken pursuant to a specific
statutory mechanism enacted for precisely such situations.
38.
For these reasons, we find that the Impugned Order's reliance on the
possibility of monetisation of unabsorbed losses...at the cost of the Government
exchequer is unsustainable, both as a matter of procedural fairness and on its own
merits, and cannot furnish a valid ground for declining restoration under Section
33(1A).
39.
ISSUE III- Whether the reasoning that the Corporate Debtor's
arbitral claim "can further proceed in the liquidation process also", and that
restoration would therefore serve no i01ndependent purpose, furnishes a
legally sound basis, on the facts of this case, for declining restoration under
Section 33(1A)?
40.
The Impugned Order's other reasoning is that since the Corporate Debtor's
only asset is, in substance, the arbitral claim against the Public Works
Department, Government of Maharashtra, valued, as recorded before the
Adjudicating Authority, at approximately Rs. 318.94 crore inclusive of litigation
costs, with interest accruing at 12.5% per annum. This was subject of a settlement
discussion pegged at approximately Rs. 363 crores before the Amicable
Settlement Committee constituted under the Vivad Se Vishwas Scheme of the
government. We are unable to sustain this reasoning, for various reasons. First, it

[PAGE 20]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1745 of 2026
Page 20 of 31

proceeds on a premise that is only partially accurate. It has been brought to our
notice that the Preliminary Report of the Liquidator dated 21.08.2026 (filed under
Regulation 13 of the IBBI (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016) itself records
that the Corporate Debtor's assets, valued in aggregate at approximately Rs.
390.48 crore of which the arbitral claim/receivable, carried as an intangible asset,
accounts for approximately Rs. 386.69 crore. This stand against aggregate
liabilities of approximately Rs. 913.82 crore. In this background whether the
claim is pursued through a resolution applicant who acquires the Corporate
Debtor or is instead realised by a liquidator through sale of the entity's remaining
assets under Section 33(5) and the Liquidation Process Regulations, the
underlying claim remain the same claim. The Impugned Order does not undertake
the process, timeline, and value likely to be realised through either route of
restoration and possible resolution of the Corporate Debtor vis-à-vis straight
liquidation of the Corporate Debtor.
41.
We must emphasise that it is well settled now that resolution and not
liquidation is the primary object of the Code, the liquidation is intended to operate
as a measure of last resort and not a default outcome to be preferred over a
genuine, credible prospect of resolution. This very principle was applied by the
Adjudicating Authority itself, on materially the same facts, in its own Extension
Order dated 02.06.2026. We also note that Section 33(1A) of the code is the
Legislature's own, express instantiation of this principle for the cases, like the
present case, where the CIRP period has run its full course without an approved

[PAGE 21]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1745 of 2026
Page 21 of 31

plan. In case of the liquidation, the creditors alone bear the ultimate economic
consequences of that choice. During the hearing, the appellant emphatically
pleaded that given a chance they will be able to achieve better outcomes rather
than the liquidation which has got its own limitations. We note that on the facts
of this case, the record discloses more than a bare, generalised hope of a better
outcome. Eleven EOIs were received against the original Form G, of which four
converted into resolution plans that were the subject of extensive negotiation over
several months. We also note the submissions made by the Appellants that since
then, seven fresh EOIs had already been received against the republished Form G
within a fortnight, five of them with the requisite EMD, and an eighth (also with
EMD) followed a day later from one of the original resolution applicants.
Although we also notice consciously that the fresh bids were received subsequent
to passing of the impugned order, and therefore the adjudicating authority did not
have benefit of the details, however since we are considering the appeal based on
the facts and the pleadings made by the appellants before us, to justify their case,
we take into consideration of these facts. In view of this, in our assessment, the
CoC's optimism about the prospect of an improved resolution outcome did not
rest on speculation. Nor, in our view, can it be said that a liquidator's realisation
of the same claim would necessarily yield comparable value. A sale of a disputed,
sub judice arbitral receivable in liquidation , necessarily discounted for litigation
risk, and further uncertain enforcement timeline. The very fact that the CoC
structured its fresh Form G to invite plans on a going-concern basis (and not

merely for assignment of the bare receivable) reflects a considered commercial
judgment, one this Appellate Tribunal will not to discount merely because, in the
abstract, a claim can be pursued either way.
42.
We would only add, that we do not read Section 33(1A) as requiring the
Adjudicating Authority, in every case, to restore the CIRP wherever the CoC
applies nor do we suggest that the mere theoretical possibility of an improved
outcome will always suffice. What we hold is that a bare, undifferentiated
statement that a monetary claim can proceed in liquidation also, without any
comparative assessment of realisable value, timelines, cost, and the market
response cannot, without more, constitute a sufficient reason to override a
unanimous, statutorily compliant CoC decision. We do not consider that
Parliament, in enacting Section 33(1A) with retrospective application to pending
CIRPs by way of the Explanation, intended the provision to be so readily rendered
nugatory.
43.
For these reasons, we find that the logic of the Adjudicating Authority that
the arbitral claim can further proceed in the liquidation process also does not, on
the facts of this case, furnish a legally sound or sufficient basis for declining
restoration under Section 33(1A). We, however, would like to make it clear that
we are not laying any general law that the Adjudicating authority can never order
for liquidation despite the COC contrary recommendations. We reiterate that all
this will depend upon the facts of each case and prevailing circumstances. In the

ISSUE VI- What relief follows?
52.
On a cumulative consideration of the foregoing, we find that the Impugned
Order cannot be sustained which it overrides a unanimous, CoC decision without
any cogent reasons and merely on a consideration that the monetisation of
unabsorbed losses which was unpleaded, unexplained, and insufficiently
supported on the record, could perhaps be misutilised. On a further consideration
that the claim can equally be pursued in liquidation that does not engage with the
comparative value-maximisation inquiry central to the object of Section 33(1A).
53.
Before concluding, we would like to reiterate that each case is required to
be dealt based on its own facts and circumstances. It is also reiterated that
liquidation is the last resort as it results in the corporate death of the corporate
entity. However, we need to keep in mind that prolonged CIRP extensions or
restoration, as the case may be, without any likely resolution of the corporate
debtor, does not also do any good to the creditors and it in this background, the
parliament has passed 2026 amendment, giving sufficient discretionary powers
to the adjudicating authority to decide on the issue of liquidation. If the facts of
any particular case warrants that liquidation is the only remedy or the whole
process seem to be vitiated based on the circumstance of the particular case, the
adjudicating authority will be within rights to determine and order for liquidation
