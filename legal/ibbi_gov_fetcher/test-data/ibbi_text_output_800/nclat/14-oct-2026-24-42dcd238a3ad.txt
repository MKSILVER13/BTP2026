Findings
67.
We note that the CIRP of the Corporate Debtor was initiated by the
Adjudicating Authority by order dated 04.06.2024 in CP(IB)/657/2021 (Manish
Taneja & Ors. v. Revital Realty Private Limited), on a petition under Section 7
of the Code. Mr. Gaurav Katiyar was appointed as the Resolution Professional of
the Corporate Debtor.

ISSUE I- Whether the Appellant's contemporaneous e-mail
communications with the Resolution Professional, in the admitted absence
of any separately maintained minutes-register, satisfy the requirement of
Regulation 16A(10)(i) read with sub-regulation (11) of the CIRP
Regulations.
84.
As noted earlier, we find that Regulation 16A(10)(i) casts on the AR a duty,
expressed in the conjunctive, both to "record proceedings" and to "prepare... the
minutes" of his meetings with the class of creditors he represents. This is a duty
distinct from, and additional to, the AR's duty under clause (b) of the same sub-
regulation merely to "review the contents of minutes prepared by the resolution
professional" for CoC meetings. In our considered opinion, the statutory
language is mandatory and the obligation is cast specific and independently upon
the AR, which reflects that the AR's consultations with his own class are a distinct
forum from the CoC meeting itself, generating a distinct documentary record.

[PAGE 36]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1736 of 2026

Page 36 of 47

85.
We also find that Sub-regulation (11) extends the provisions regarding
minutes of meetings in this regulation, mutatis mutandis, to clause (10)(i). We
need to appreciate that Regulation 16A does not itself elsewhere prescribe the
content of "minutes" save by the oblique reference, in clause (10)(b), to minutes
"prepared by the resolution professional”, which are, in substance, the minutes of
the CoC meeting governed by Regulation 24. On a purposive reading, sub-
regulation (11) is best understood as importing, mutatis mutandis, the standard
applicable to CoC minutes under Regulation 24 into the AR's record of his own
class-consultations. We find that Code or Regulations prescribes no specific
form, template, register or signature requirement; it requires, however, that the
minutes "disclose the particulars of the participants" and be reasonably promptly
generated and available.
86.
In view of this regulatory regime, we are of view that the absence of a
prescribed form is not synonymous with the absence of any prescribed content.
Applying Regulation 24's standard mutatis mutandis, an AR's record of a class-
consultation meeting should, at a minimum, identify: (a) that the
meeting/consultation was held and its date; (b) the number, or at least a
representative indication, of the class members who participated; and (c) the
substance of the concerns/views expressed. On the record before this Appellate
Tribunal, the Appellant's contemporaneous e-mails to the Resolution Professional
appear, prima facie, to satisfy limbs (a) and (c) with reasonable consistency across
the CIRP. What is conspicuously absent from the record is any consistent

[PAGE 37]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1736 of 2026

Page 37 of 47

indication of limb (b) — the number or representative character of the
homebuyers who in fact attended or were consulted at each preliminary meeting.
That omission is not a trivial one where, as here, the class numbered upward of a
thousand geographically dispersed persons: a preliminary meeting attended by an
unrepresentative handful, whose views are then transmitted to the RP as the
position of the homebuyers, is a real and not merely theoretical risk which the
recording requirement under Regulation 16A(10)(i) is, in part, designed to guard
against and make verifiable.
87.
At the same time, it is a relevant and mitigating circumstance going to the
gravity, though not to the existence, of the contravention that this specific
allegation did not form part of the original complaint but was raised suo motu in
the course of the Board's own examination, a circumstance which, while not
ousting the Board's jurisdiction under Section 218 of the Code to act on matters
coming to its notice, calls for care in weighing whether the Appellant had, from
the outset of the examination, a fair and specific opportunity to meet a case of
continuing default spanning the entirety of a nearly two-year CIRP; further we
note that the IBBI's own examination proceeded by calling upon the Appellant to
produce the written records of the meetings/consultations, which he did, on the
very day such records were sought (09.04.2026), indicating a degree of
cooperation, and an absence of concealment, inconsistent with a wilful or
contumacious disregard of the Regulation.

Regulation 16A(10)(i) read with sub-regulation (11) is made out, in that the

concerns raised. This finding is, however, one of incomplete documentation of an
admittedly and consistently conducted consultation process, rather than of an
absence of consultation, or of concealment, or of any consequence traceable to a

ISSUE II- Whether the Appellant's concurrence, without prior
instructions of the class of creditors, to the extensions of 17.03.2026 and
19.03.2026 for submission of revised resolution plans, contravened Section
25A (3) of the Code.
90.
We note that Section 25A (1) of the Code confines the AR's obligation to
cast an instructed vote to "meetings of the committee of creditors," in accordance
with "prior voting instructions... obtained through physical or electronic means."
Sub-section (3), on which the Impugned Order rests, provides that the AR shall
not act against the interest of the financial creditor he represents and shall always
act in accordance with their prior instructions. The word "always" is emphatic,
and on a purely textual reading admits no carve-out for matters the AR may
subjectively regard as procedural rather than substantive. To this extent, the IBBI
findings that an AR has no free-standing discretion to reclassify a matter as

[PAGE 39]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1736 of 2026

Page 39 of 47

procedural in order to excuse himself from seeking instructions finds support in
the plain language of the provision, and on this we are also inclined to hold the
same view as a matter of statutory construction. We need to appreciate that the
extension impugned here was not the subject of any resolution moved or voted
upon at a CoC meeting, rather at best it was an administrative timeline extension
granted by the Resolution Professional to PRAs, upon which the Appellant along
with other CoC members informally consulted, offered a view by e-mail. This
does not, in our assessment, place the matter beyond the reach of Section 25A(3)
altogether as the sub-section is not, on its language, confined only to formal votes
but it is a circumstance materially bearing on the character and gravity of any
default in obtaining instructions, since what was communicated was in the nature
of an opinion on an Resolution Professional-driven procedural step, not a vote
determining the fate of a resolution plan, the distribution of proceeds, or any other
matter substantively affecting creditors' entitlements.
91.
We would also like to take into consideration two further circumstances
which are of significance in present case. First, the extension of 19.03.2026 to
23.03.2026 was occasioned by an intervening event i.e. the EPFO's claim for
provident fund dues which is not shown to have been foreseeable when the CoC
met on 06.03.2026 and fixed the original timeline. The Appellant's concurrence
was directed at ensuring that a statutory due claim (itself for the ultimate benefit
of workmen), was properly addressed in the revised plans, rather than at
conferring any advantage on a PRA. Second, the Appellant has pointed to the

[PAGE 40]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1736 of 2026

Page 40 of 47

practical difficulty of convening a fresh round of formal consultation and voting
involving notice, an agenda, a voting window, and collation of results for a class
of 1,428 dispersed creditors within the few working days available before the
extended deadline, a submission which is not without force, though it cannot by
itself dispense with a statutory requirement, if one otherwise applies.
92.
We find it relevant factor weighed against the countervailing consideration,
correctly emphasised by the IBBI that permitting an AR to determine unilaterally
which matters warrant prior consultation and which do not would substantially
erode the very check that Section 25A was designed to install , particularly where,
the AR's class commanded 91% of the CoC's voting share, such that the AR's
individual view, however well-intentioned, effectively substituted for the will of
a decisive majority of creditors on a matter (timeline extension enabling PRAs to
compete for resolution of the Corporate Debtor) not without consequence for the
eventual resolution outcome. It is also material that the Appellant, on his own
pleaded practice, held preview meetings before conveying views to the RP on
other CoC-agenda items; no corresponding preview meeting preceding the
communications of 17.03.2026 or 19.03.2026 specifically has been shown,
suggesting an inconsistency between the Appellant's general practice and his
conduct on the two occasions actually in issue.
93.
On balance, we find that the Appellant's concurrence to the extensions of
17.03.2026 and 19.03.2026, without prior instructions of the class of homebuyers,
does contravene Section 25A(3) of the Code, the provision admitting of no textual

[PAGE 41]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1736 of 2026

Page 41 of 47

exception for matters an AR characterises as procedural. This finding is, however,
qualified by the then prevailing circumstances, again relevant to gravity rather
than to the existence of the contravention. We need to appreciate the narrow,
time-bound, administrative character of the extension in its Resolution
Professional-driven origin and further its connection to a statutory due claim of
EPFO. It also favours the arguments of the Appellant that it was done for genuine
reasons for considerations of EPFO dues rather than to any commercial
evaluation of a resolution plan and further the absence of any resolution actually
voted upon by the CoC. We give suitable weightage to the fact that there is
complete absence of any suggestion that any PRA was thereby favoured, or that
the ultimate resolution outcome was altered to the detriment of the class
represented by the Appellant.

ISSUE III- Whether, even assuming both contraventions to be
established, the penalty of cancellation of registration and the consequential
disqualification from the Appellant's other, unconnected assignments, is
proportionate to the gravity of the established misconduct.
95.
We shall now deal the crucial question of penalty which may be distinct
from the question of contraventions per se, although we are of opinion that
penalty depend on established contraventions. We take into consideration that
the doctrine of proportionality, as a facet of judicial/appellate review of
disciplinary punishment, was authoritatively stated by Honourable Supreme

[PAGE 42]

Page 42 of 47

Court in case of Ranjit Thakur v. Union of India, (1987) 4 SCC 611, where the
Supreme Court held:
"Judicial review generally speaking, is not directed against a
decision, but is directed against the 'decision-making process'. The
question of the choice and quantum of punishment is within the
jurisdiction and discretion of the [disciplinary authority]. But the
sentence has to suit the offence and the offender. It should not be
vindictive or unduly harsh. It should not be so disproportionate to
the offence as to shock the conscience and amount in itself to
conclusive evidence of bias. The doctrine of proportionality, as
part of the concept of judicial review, would ensure that even on
an aspect which is, otherwise, within the exclusive province of [the
disciplinary authority], if the decision... even as to sentence is an
outrageous defiance of logic, then the sentence would not be
immune from correction. Irrationality and perversity are
recognised grounds of judicial review."
96.

From above judgements of the Honourable Supreme Court, we find that
two propositions of direct relevance emerge relevant in the present appeal before
us. The first, that a reviewing forum will interfere with the quantum of a
disciplinary penalty where it is so disproportionate to the proven misconduct as
to shock the conscience or amount to an outrageous defiance of logic and the
second, that the ordinarily appropriate remedy on such interference is remand to
the disciplinary authority for fresh consideration of penalty, this Appellate
Tribunal itself substituting a penalty only in rare and exceptional cases, typically
to shorten litigation.
