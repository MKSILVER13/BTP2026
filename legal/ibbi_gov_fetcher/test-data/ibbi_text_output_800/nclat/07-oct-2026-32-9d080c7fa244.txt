33.
No oral agreement for creation of financial debt: It is submitted that No
Oral Agreement of any kind was reached between the Appellant and the
Respondent. The Appellant has not provided any date of any oral agreement,
nor has provided with whom the Appellant, had entered into such oral
agreement. It is well settled position of law that companies which are artificial
persons, cannot enter into any oral contract under the provisions of the
Companies Act. No financial contract exists between the Appellant and the
Respondent as stated in Form -1 filed before Ld. NCLT. Oral contract can be
enforced and verified only by leading evidence before a civil court and cannot
be done before NCLT as held by this Hon'ble NCLAT in the case Prayag
Polytech VS Bon Lon Petrochem LLP. In Jagdambey International Vs Visa
Powertech (Para-4), Hon'ble NCLAT has held that mere oral transaction
without any documentary evidence and mere reliance on balance sheet is not
sufficient for creation of a Financial Debt. The Appellant has not indicated as
to when the oral Contract was executed and by whom the said alleged oral
contract was entered. No details have been given regarding the board
resolutions of the respective companies authorizing execution for the alleged
financial transaction/contract. It is submitted that the Section 21 of the
Companies Act, 2013 (called hereinafter as "Act of 2013") provides as under:
"21. Authentication of documents, proceedings and Contracts – Save as
otherwise provided in the Act-(a) A document or proceeding requiring
authentication by a company; or (b) contracts made by or on behalf of a

[PAGE 18]

company, may be signed by any key managerial personnel or an office of
the company duty authorized by the board in this behalf ".

From the aforesaid provision it is abundantly clear that any proceedings and
contract executed by a company needs an authentication as above. In the
present matter the Appellant has not produced any document in the nature
of a financial contract as defined under the Rules of 2016. Further in view of

Points for determination.
44.
The following points arise for our consideration:
(i)
Whether the sum of ₹ 1,39,00,000/- paid by the Appellant to the
Respondent is a "financial debt" under Section 5(8) of the Code, so
that the Appellant is a "financial creditor" under Section 5(7) of the
Code in respect of it.

[PAGE 26]

(ii) Whether the errors pointed out by the Appellant in the Impugned

A. Scope of Section 7 of the Code
45.
Section 7 allows a financial creditor to apply for initiation of CIRP when
a default has occurred. Two things must therefore be shown. The first is that
a financial debt is owed to the applicant. The second is that there is a default
in payment of that debt. The Appellant relies on Swiss Ribbons (supra) and
Innoventive Industries (supra), and in its written submissions also on
Elegna (supra), Omkara (supra) and A. Navinchandra Steels (supra), for the
proposition that once debt and default are proved, the application is to be
admitted and no dispute can be raised to defeat it. The proposition is not in
doubt. That rule, however, applies only after a financial debt is established.
The burden of proving it is on the Appellant. The first question is thus whether
the amount claimed is a financial debt.

46.
Section 5(8) of the Code opens as follows:
"financial debt" means a debt alongwith interest, if any, which
is disbursed against the consideration for the time value of
money and includes—

Section 5(7) says that a "financial creditor" means any person to whom a
financial debt is owed. The key words in Section 5(8) are "disbursed against
the consideration for the time value of money". The Appellant itself accepts
this test. It relies on Pawan Kumar (supra), where this Appellate Tribunal has
held the following to be essential conditions to be satisfied by a Financial
Creditor under Section 7 read with Section 5 (7) and (8) of the Code:

[PAGE 27]
Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1448 of 2022 27 of 39

a. There must be disbursal of loan amount.
b. Such disbursal should be made for a consideration for time
value of money, and
c. When the debt (whole or any part or installment) become
due and payable and is not paid by the Corporate Debtor
means committed default.

B. The nature of the original payment
47.
The following facts are not in dispute. A tripartite MOU was signed on
27.08.2014 by the Appellant, the Respondent and the shareholders of the
Respondent. The shareholders agreed to sell their entire shareholding to the
Appellant. The Appellant paid ₹ 1,39,00,000/- to the Respondent by six
cheques dated 29.08.2014 to 31.08.2014. The Respondent admits receipt of
the money. The share deal was not completed, and the Appellant itself
describes the position as a stalemate.

48.
The clauses of the MOU, as placed before us by the Respondent, show
what the money was meant for:
2. CONSIDERATION
The transferors hereby agrees to sell and transfer to the Transferee and/or
their nominee/s and the Transferee hereby agree to purchase and acquire
from the Transferors all the Sale Shares held by the Transferors in "GGPL"
free from all encumbrances, charges, items, liens and claims, and on the
terms and conditions recorded herein.
The Transferee agrees to purchases the shares of a price work out on the
basis of Net worth of the company & in addition of it, agrees to discharge
the following obligation.
(a) Payment of Term Loan as on 31st August 2014
(b) Payment of Unsecured Loan as on 31st August 2014.

[PAGE 28]
Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1448 of 2022 28 of 39

(c) Payment of other current liabilities.
The consideration for said has been workout at ₹ 770.00 lakh and includes
the following.
a) Payment of Term Loan as on 31st August 2014
b) Payment of Unsecured Loan as on 31st August 2014.
c) Payment of Other Current Liabilities
d) Balance towards payment towards purchases of shares.

49.
These clauses deal with the price of the shares and the way it was to be
applied. Part of it was to clear the liabilities of the Respondent, and the
balance was to go towards the shares. The MOU does not say that the
Respondent will repay any sum to the Appellant. It does not provide for
interest or for any other return for the use of money over time. The Appellant's
own pleading is to the same effect. It says that the payment was made "as
part payment under the Memorandum of Understanding towards the
acquisition of shares". When the cheques were issued, then, the money was
not "disbursed against the consideration for the time value of money". It was
paid as a part of the price in a share purchase.

50.
The Appellant contends that money received by a company from an
intending purchaser of shares can only be share application money or an
unsecured loan, and cannot be a part of the price payable to the shareholders.
We are unable to accept this. The MOU was a tripartite arrangement to which
the Respondent was itself a party. The parties agreed that the price of ₹ 770
lakhs would include the discharge of the Respondent's term loan, unsecured
loans and other current liabilities, and that only the balance would go towards
the shares. Payment to the Respondent was thus the mode agreed by all three

[PAGE 29]
Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1448 of 2022 29 of 39

parties for applying a part of the price. Even on the Appellant's own
description, share application money is paid for the allotment of shares and
not as a loan. On neither description was the money disbursed against the
consideration for the time value of money.
C. The alleged oral agreement
51.
The Appellant says that the nature of the payment changed after the
deal failed. Its case is that an oral agreement was reached and the payment
"may be continued as an unsecured loan with interest payable at 11% per
annum repayable on demand". The Appellant admits that "No formal
agreement was executed between the Financial Creditor and the Corporate
Debtor". The Respondent denies any such oral agreement.

52.
The Appellant has not stated when the oral agreement was made. It has
not said who spoke for each company, and it has not shown any board
resolution or other authority for it. The Special Resolution dated 25.08.2014
and the Board Resolution dated 26.08.2014, on which the Appellant relies in
its written submissions, are dated before the MOU itself. They cannot evidence
an oral agreement said to have been reached after the share transaction failed.
No document signed or accepted by both parties, which sets out the rate of
interest, the tenure or the date of repayment, has been shown to us. The
Respondent relies on Jagdambey International v. Visa Powertech (supra)
(Para 4) and Prayag Polytech v. Bon Lon Petrochem LLP (supra) to submit
that a mere oral transaction, without documentary evidence and with only a
balance sheet to support it, cannot create a financial debt.

[PAGE 30]
Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1448 of 2022 30 of 39

53.
We do not prescribe for that an oral agreement can never support a
claim of financial debt. We hold that on the record the Appellant has not
shown that such an agreement was made and what its terms were. A Section
7 application is decided on documents and admitted facts in a summary
manner. A disputed oral agreement, whose existence and terms depend on
the word of one side against the other, cannot be tried in such a proceeding.

54.
The authorities relied on by the Appellant do not assist it. In Ravi Auto
(supra) the disbursement was admitted and the Corporate Debtor did not
deny paying interest on that. In Agarwal Polysacks (supra) the Adjudicating
Authority had itself found that the disbursement carried interest and was
repayable on demand. In Satish Balan (supra), where the plea was that the
money was equity, the Corporate Debtor had paid interest for two years,
issued confirmations stating the interest payable, and could produce nothing
in support of its plea. In Pradeep Tayal (supra) the Corporate Debtor had
admitted the loan in reply to the demand notice. In Pancham Studios (supra),
the plea of the Corporate Debtor that the amount represented its managing
director's share in a family property was not supported by any document
recording that the amount was paid for that purpose, and the amount had
been shown continuously in its balance sheets as an unsecured loan. In each
case the transaction was shown to be a loan from the outset. None involved a
written agreement, executed by the creditor itself, showing that the money
was paid for a different purpose. Here the MOU does exactly that.

[PAGE 31]
Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1448 of 2022 31 of 39

55.
The Respondent has also pointed out that the Appellant's own balance
sheet as on 31st March 2015 stated as follows:
"There is no loans, secured or unsecured, granted by the company to the
companies, firms or other parties, covered in the register maintained
under Section 189 of the Companies Act, 2013."

That statement is limited to loans covered by the register under Section 189,
so we do not give it much weight. Our decision does not rest on it.
D. Balance sheets, balance confirmations and TDS
56.
The main material relied on by the Appellant is the conduct of the
Respondent. The Respondent showed the amount as "Unsecured Loan
Received" in its balance sheets, gave balance confirmation letters on
30.06.2015 and 15.06.2017, credited interest, and deducted TDS under
Section 194A of the Income Tax Act, 1961. The Respondent does not deny
these entries. The Impugned Order also notes that TDS was deducted.

57.
The question under Section 5(8) is what the money was disbursed for.
It is not how the debtor chose to record it later. An entry made by one party
in its own books cannot change the nature of a payment made earlier for a
different purpose. The Appellant relies on Mahabir Cold Storage (supra) and
on Asset Reconstruction Company (India) Ltd. (supra) for the view that a
balance sheet can amount to an acknowledgment of debt for the purposes of
Section 18 of the Limitation Act, 1963. That is the settled position. That an
acknowledgment shows only, that some money is owed. But that itself does
not show that the money is a "financial debt" as defined in Section 5(8). The
Respondent is right in saying that merely showing an amount as an

[PAGE 32]
Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1448 of 2022 32 of 39

unsecured loan does not by itself create the relationship of Financial Creditor
and Corporate Debtor.

58.
The Appellant points out that, as recorded in para 4(e) of the Impugned
Order, the Respondent's own case was that the money was "Share Application
Money converted into unsecured Loan", and contends that the Respondent
cannot accept the money, admit the conversion and still deny the relationship
of creditor and debtor. Read as a whole, the Respondent's stand is that the
amount was shown as an unsecured loan in its books for accounting
purposes. A change in the head under which an amount is shown records
how it is classified. It does not by itself establish that the parties agreed that
the money would be retained against a return for its use. That agreement is
what the Appellant must prove, and, as held above, it has not done so.

59.
On TDS, both sides have placed NCLAT decisions before us. The
Appellant relies on Pradeep Tayal (supra), in which it is said that:
"the deduction of TDS and deposit by the Respondent/ Corporate Debtor
does not itself prove that there is any financial debt but deduction of TDS
and deposit in FORM 16-A under section 194A of the Income Tax Act clearly
proves that the deduction which was deposited was TDS relating to
"Interest other than interest on securities."

The Respondent relies on Prayag Polytech v. Gem Batteries (supra) (Para
6), where it was observed:
"Merely pointing out that TDS was deducted would not be sufficient to
conclude that there was financial Debt. "

[PAGE 33]
Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1448 of 2022 33 of 39

The two observations are not in conflict. At the highest, TDS under Section
194A shows that the debtor treated a payment or credit as interest. It does
not prove that the principal was lent for a return, or that the parties agreed
on interest. That has to be shown by other material.

60.
There is more here. The Appellant itself relies on Para 16 of the
Impugned Order, which says:
“Although, the TDS was deducted and accounted for in his books of
accounts by the Corporate Debtor, the Corporate Debtor never deposited
the accrued interest on the loan amount in bank account of the Financial
Creditor.”

So, by the Appellant's own case, apart from the tax deducted at source, no
part of the interest was ever paid to it. What exists is a set of entries in the
Respondent's books. No conduct of the parties, such as actual payment of
interest, supports the claim that the money was lent for a return. Entries of
this kind cannot create a financial debt that did not arise when the money
was paid. While we are aware, as held in Orator Marketing Pvt. Ltd. v.
Samtex Desinz Pvt. Ltd., (2023) 3 SCC 753, relied on by the Appellant, that
the payment or stipulation of interest is not an essential element of a financial
debt, in the present case the very nature of the transaction is in question,
since the MOU shows that the money was paid as part of the price of shares
and not as a loan. The absence of any actual payment of interest is relevant
only as a circumstance confirming that nature.

[PAGE 34]
Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1448 of 2022 34 of 39

61.
The Appellant also submits that these documents formed part of the
record of an information utility. Such a record evidences the information
submitted, and may serve as proof of default under Section 7(3)(a) of the Code.
It does not decide the character of a disbursement which the corporate debtor
disputes. Nothing has been shown to indicate that the Respondent
authenticated the record so as to accept that the amount was a financial debt.
E. Time value of money
62.
The Respondent has relied on Sanjay Kewalramani (supra), where the
NCLAT held as under:
"There is nothing on the record to suggest that 2nd and 3rd Respondents
had given the loan in favour of the Corporate Debtor, which can be termed
to be disbursement of an amount for consideration for the time value of
money as required under Section 5 (8). Merely grant of loan and admission
of taking loan will ipso facto not treat the 2nd and 3rd Respondents as
Financial Creditors, till they show that it complies with the substantive
definition or any one or other clause of Section 5 (8)."

The same reasoning applies here. The MOU has no clause on the time value
of money. The alleged oral agreement is not proved. The balance sheets,
confirmations and TDS entries do not fill that gap. Nothing on record shows
that the Appellant parted with ₹ 1,39,00,000/- in order to earn a return on it.
The money went to the Respondent as a part of the price in a share deal,
which then failed.

63.
Each side blames the other for the failure of the deal. The Appellant
says that the shareholders and the Respondent did not complete their
obligations. The Respondent says that the Appellant did not pay the full

[PAGE 35]
Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1448 of 2022 35 of 39

consideration of ₹ 770 lakhs. Whether the Appellant can recover the money,
and with what interest, depends on the terms of the MOU, on what the parties
did afterwards, and on evidence. These matters need a full trial. They cannot
be decided under Section 7 of the Code.

64.
We find that the sum of ₹ 1,39,00,000/- is not a financial debt within
Section 5(8) of the Code. The Appellant is therefore not a financial creditor in
respect of it under Section 5(7). We agree with the Adjudicating Authority that
the Section 7 application is not maintainable. Point (i) is answered against the
Appellant.
F. Other objections of the Respondent
65.
The Respondent has also argued that there is no financial contract
under Rule 3(1)(d) of the 2016 Rules, no board resolution or register of loans
under Section 21 and Section 186(9) of the Companies Act, 2013, and no
default under Section 3(12) of the Code. The Appellant has argued that a
written agreement is not a pre-requisite under the Code. In its written
submissions, the Appellant has also relied on Pancham Studios (supra), the
doctrine of indoor management as explained in MRF Ltd. (supra) and Section
238 of the Code, and on the Special Resolution dated 25.08.2014 and the
Board Resolution dated 26.08.2014, to contend that non-compliance with
Section 186 cannot defeat a debt. It has further addressed the variation in the
rate of interest from year to year and the time taken to issue the demand
notice. We have not decided these questions, because the Appeal fails at the
first step. Our decision does not rest on Section 186, on the absence of any
register or resolution, on the rate of interest or on delay, and we express no

[PAGE 36]
Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1448 of 2022 36 of 39

view on these contentions or on the reasoning of the Adjudicating Authority
on them. We note only that the Appellant's own papers give the date of the
demand notice both as 20.02.2019 and as 27.02.2019. Since there is no
financial debt, we do not need to settle which date is correct or whether a
default as defined in Section 3(12) took place.
G. The errors alleged in the Impugned Order
66.
The Appellant points out that the Impugned Order wrongly records its
counsel as counsel for the Respondent. It also says that its written
submissions filed on 14.09.2022 were wrongly described as those of the
Corporate Debtor. These are mistakes of description. We have examined the
pleadings, documents and written submissions of both sides afresh, and our
decision rests on that examination. No prejudice to the Appellant has been
shown. These mistakes do not justify setting aside an order which is correct
on merits. If the Appellant wants them corrected, it may apply to the
Adjudicating Authority. Point (ii) is answered accordingly.
H. Conduct of the Respondent
67.
We cannot end without noting the conduct of the Respondent. It
received ₹ 1,39,00,000/- and kept it for years. In its own books it showed the
amount as an unsecured loan, credited interest on it and deducted tax at
source. Yet, as recorded in the Impugned Order, it never paid the interest to
the Appellant. According to the Appellant, after the legal notice the
Respondent also stopped showing the amount separately in its books. The
Respondent thus credited interest and deducted tax at source on it year after
year on a liability which it now says never existed, while the interest itself was
never paid to the Appellant. Whether this treatment is consistent with the

[PAGE 37]
Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1448 of 2022 37 of 39

Income-tax Act, 1961, as applicable to the relevant years, is a matter for the
income-tax authorities to examine.

68.
We make no finding on these matters, as they are not before us for
decision. The Appellant is free to raise them before the appropriate authorities
and fora. Since the matter concerns the administration of tax, we consider it
appropriate that it be brought to the notice of the income-tax authorities.
Accordingly, in exercise of the inherent powers of this Tribunal under Rule 11
of the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal Rules, 2016, the Registry
shall forward a copy of this judgment to the Principal Commissioner of Income
Tax having jurisdiction over the Respondent, for such action, if any, as may
be considered appropriate in accordance with law. Nothing in this judgment
shall be construed as an expression of opinion on any liability of the
Respondent under the income-tax law.
Conclusion.
69.
Our conclusions are as follows:
(a) The Appellant paid ₹ 1,39,00,000/- to the Respondent under the
MOU dated 27.08.2014, as a part of the price in a share purchase.
The MOU does not provide for repayment or for any return on the
money.

(b) The Appellant has not proved the alleged oral agreement that
converted the payment into a loan with interest at 11% per annum,
repayable on demand.

[PAGE 38]
Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1448 of 2022 38 of 39

(c)
The Respondent's balance sheets, balance confirmations and TDS
entries do not, by themselves, prove that the money was disbursed
against the consideration for the time value of money. By the
Appellant's own case, apart from the tax deducted at source, no
interest was actually paid to it, which is one more circumstance
showing the true nature of the payment.

(d) The amount is therefore not a "financial debt" under Section 5(8) of
the Code, and the Appellant is not a "financial creditor" in respect of
it. The Section 7 application was rightly dismissed.

(e)
The errors in the Impugned Order alleged by the Appellant are
clerical and have caused no prejudice.

(f)
The money paid by the Appellant remains with the Respondent. The
Appellant may have a civil remedy for it, but that remedy cannot be
worked out under the Code.

Company Appeal (AT) (Ins) No. 1448 of 2022, is dismissed. The Appellant is
