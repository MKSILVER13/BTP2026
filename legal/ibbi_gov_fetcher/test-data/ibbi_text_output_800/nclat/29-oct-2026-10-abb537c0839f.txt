NARESH SALECHA, MEMBER (TECHNICAL)
1.
The present appeal has been filed by the Appellant i.e., Shobhit J. Rajan,
who is the Promoter/Suspended Director of Ecstasy Realty Private Limited
(“Corporate Debtor”), under Section 61 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy
Code, 2016 (‘Code’) against the Order dated 14.08.2026 ("Impugned Order")
passed by the National Company Law Tribunal, Mumbai Bench ("Adjudicating
Authority") in IA No. 2785 of 2026 in Company Petition (IB) No. 922 of 2022.
2.
M/s. ARCK Resolution Professionals LLP, who is the Resolution
Professional of the Corporate Debtor, is the Respondent No.1 herein.
3.
Committee of Creditors (Catalyst Trusteeship Limited) of Ecstasy Realty
Private Limited, is Respondent No. 2, herein.
4.
The Appellant submitted that the Corporate Debtor is engaged in real estate
construction and development and he has nearly 33 years of experience in the real
estate industry. The Corporate Debtor was registered as a Micro, Small and

[PAGE 3]
Page 3 of 41

Medium Enterprise (MSME) on 27.02.2025. The Corporate Debtor was admitted
into the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) on 09.03.2026 in C.P.
No. 922/2022. Thereafter, Respondent No. 1, the Resolution Professional,
published Form G on 08.05.2026.
5.
The Appellant submitted that, under the Detailed Expression of Interest
(EOI), the initial deadline for submission of EOI was 23.05.2026, which was
subsequently extended to 04.06.2026. The timeline for submission of resolution
plans, together with the requisite Earnest Money Deposit (EMD), was thereafter
extended up to 24.08.2026, as recorded in the fourth and fifth meetings of the
Committee of Creditors (CoC).
6.
The Appellant contended that he submitted his EOI on 22.05.2026, within
the prescribed timeline and in the prescribed format, in his capacity as the original
promoter of the MSME Corporate Debtor. Along with the EOI, he deposited an
EMD of Rs. 5 Crores into the designated bank account of the Corporate Debtor.
He further furnished his Income Tax Returns for Assessment Years 2023–24,
2024–25 and 2025–26 and disclosed his CIBIL score of 776 as on 21.05.2026.
According to the Appellant, these documents demonstrated his bona fides,
financial seriousness and willingness to undertake the revival of the Corporate
Debtor.
7.
The Appellant submitted that, on 10.06.2026, Respondent No. 1 called
upon him to furnish a net-worth certificate or explain the reasons for its non-
submission. Instead of proceeding with the EOI on the basis of the documents

[PAGE 4]
Page 4 of 41

already furnished, Respondent No. 1 placed the EOI under verification solely on
account of the absence of the net-worth certificate. The Appellant contended that
this requirement was intended for prospective resolution applicants (PRAs) and
ought not to have been mechanically applied to him, considering his distinct
status as the original promoter of the MSME Corporate Debtor.
8.
The Appellant stated that, by his letter dated 18.06.2026, he informed
Respondent No. 1 that his entitlement to submit a resolution proposal was
required to be considered in the context of Section 240A of the Code, the
legislative intent underlying the MSME framework and the distinction between
an existing promoter and an unrelated third-party resolution applicant. He
contended that the eligibility criteria prescribed under Section 25(2)(h) of the
code and Regulation 36A of the CIRP Regulations ought not to be applied to him
in a manner that foreclosed his participation at the threshold.
9.
The Appellant further submitted that, in his letter dated 18.06.2026, he
reiterated his deposit of Rs. 5 crores towards EMD, submission of the aforesaid
Income Tax Returns and disclosure of his CIBIL score of 776. He maintained that
the Resolution Professional had failed to consider these particulars while
examining his EOI. The Appellant also undertook to furnish the source and proof
of funds and, if required, a comfort letter, and expressed his willingness to honour
the financial commitments contained in his proposal.
10.
The Appellant contended that Respondent No. 1 rejected his EOI by letter
dated 22.06.2026, solely on the ground of non-submission of the net-worth

[PAGE 5]
Page 5 of 41

certificate. According to the Appellant, the rejection was affected without
examining the merits of his proposal or placing the same before the CoC for
consideration of its viability, feasibility and potential for maximisation of the
Corporate Debtor’s value.
11.
Aggrieved thereby, the Appellant filed I.A. No. 2785/2026 before the
Adjudicating Authority, seeking to set aside the rejection letter dated 22.06.2026.
The Appellant submitted that the replies filed by Respondent No. 1 and the CoC
demonstrated that his proposal had not been examined on its merits and had
instead been rejected at the threshold for want of the net-worth certificate. By the
impugned order dated 14.08.2026, the Adjudicating Authority dismissed the
application, inter alia, holding that the Appellant was not exempted from the
eligibility criteria prescribed under Section 25(2)(h) of the code.
12.
The Appellant contended that he does not claim an unconditional right to
regain control of the Corporate Debtor or an automatic entitlement to approval of
his resolution proposal. His limited case is that, in the exceptional circumstances
pleaded, he ought to have been permitted to place his proposal before the CoC
for consideration on its merits. According to the Appellant, the CoC should have
been afforded an opportunity to assess the proposal on the touchstones of
feasibility, viability and value maximisation rather than excluding him solely for
want of a net-worth certificate.
13.
The Appellant submitted that Section 25(2)(h) of the code empowers the
Resolution Professional to invite prospective resolution applicants who fulfil the

[PAGE 6]
Page 6 of 41

eligibility criteria laid down with the approval of the CoC. He contended that the
expression “invite” must be interpreted in the context of the statutory scheme,
which, according to him, distinguishes an existing promoter of an MSME
Corporate Debtor from unrelated third-party applicants seeking to acquire control
of the Corporate Debtor. He maintained that treating the promoter identically to
an outsider for every procedural requirement would defeat the legislative
objective of facilitating the revival of MSMEs.
14.
The Appellant relied upon Section 240A of the code and the legislative
developments reflected in the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment)
Act, 2018, the Insolvency Law Committee Report, 2018, and the IBC (Second
Amendment) Act, 2018. The Appellant contended that the legislative framework
recognises the need to facilitate the participation of MSME promoters in the
resolution process and that the eligibility criteria prescribed for PRAs cannot be
applied mechanically so as to render such participation illusory. The Appellant
submitted that the absence of an express relaxation under Regulation 36A(4) of
the CIRP Regulations should not be construed as extinguishing the independent
protection which, according to him, flows from Section 240A of the code.
15.
The Appellant clarified that he does not seek exemption from the
substantive requirements governing a resolution plan, including the
disqualification contemplated under Section 29A and compliance with Section
30(4) of the code. Rather, he seeks an opportunity to submit his proposal so that

[PAGE 7]
Page 7 of 41

its financial viability, feasibility and value-maximisation potential may be
evaluated by the CoC in accordance with law.
16.
The Appellant contended that the Adjudicating Authority had erroneously
relied upon R. Raghavendran v. C. Raja John & Ors., [(2023) 241 Comp Cas
1], by reading the judgment in a restrictive manner. The Appellant submitted that
the setting aside of paragraphs 32–34 of C. Raja John v. R. Raghavendran, RP
of Springfield Shelters Pvt. Ltd., [(2021) ibclaw\.in 563 NCLAT], could not be
interpreted as laying down an inflexible rule that an MSME promoter must
invariably be excluded at the threshold without consideration of the particular
facts and circumstances of the case.
17.
The Appellant relied upon Saravana Global Holdings Ltd. & Anr. v.
Bafna Pharmaceuticals Ltd. & Ors., 2019 SCC OnLine NCLAT 962, and
submitted that the decision recognises the possibility of an MSME promoter
being permitted to participate in the resolution process in exceptional
circumstances without necessarily competing with unrelated PRAs. He
contended that the promoter’s proposal must ultimately satisfy the CoC regarding
viability, feasibility and value maximisation. He further relied upon Swiss
Ribbons Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India, [(2019) 213 Comp Cas 198 (SC)], in support
of his submission that the CIRP framework must be interpreted consistently with
its underlying objectives.
18.
The Appellant also relied upon the decision in Lomat International NV v.
Wearit Global Limited, I.A. (IB) No. 53/KB/2024 in C.P. (IB) No. 100/KB/2019,

[PAGE 8]
Page 8 of 41

wherein, according to him, the Adjudicating Authority considered relaxation of
the net-worth criterion for an MSME promoter while retaining the requirement
of depositing the applicable security deposit and EMD. The Appellant relied upon
the following principle recorded in the said decision: an MSME promoter may be
granted relaxation from the net-worth criterion, but such relaxation does not
dispense with the requirement of depositing the security deposit at the EOI stage
and the EMD at the resolution-plan stage.
19.
The Appellant contended that the aforesaid decision was directly relevant
because he had already deposited an EMD of Rs. 5 crores and furnished financial
and credit particulars in support of his bona fides. He submitted that the
circumstances of his case warranted consideration of a similar relaxation,
particularly because his proposal had been rejected without examination of its
merits. He further maintained that neither Bafna Pharmaceuticals (supra) nor C.
Raja John (supra) prescribed an exhaustive definition of “exceptional
circumstances” that would preclude consideration of the facts pleaded by him.
20.
The Appellant submitted that the exceptional circumstances in the present
case arise cumulatively from the various factors: The Corporate Debtor is an
MSME; the Appellant is its original promoter; he submitted his EOI within the
prescribed period and deposited Rs. 5 crores towards EMD; he furnished his
Income Tax Returns and credit particulars and undertook to disclose the source
and proof of funds; he relied upon repayment of approximately Rs.182 crores
towards the Corporate Debtor’s debt under his guidance and control; and his EOI

the CoC for consideration on merits. He contended that these circumstances
warranted an assessment of his proposal rather than an automatic threshold
rejection.

22.
The Appellant contended that the requirement of a standalone net-worth

a substantial contributing asset to his net worth before CIRP. He submitted that,

his ability to arrange the requisite funding through borrowing, investors, funding

39.

40.
The Respondent No. 1 submitted that the order dated 14 August 2026

41.
The Respondent No. 1 submitted that the Appellant had no vested or
indefeasible right to submit a resolution plan or claim consideration as a
prospective resolution applicant without satisfying the prescribed eligibility
criteria. It is contended that the Appellant’s erstwhile promoter status did not
confer any overriding entitlement to bypass the requirements under Section
25(2)(h) of the Code. In support of this submission, Respondent No. 1 relied upon
Swiss Ribbons Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India, (2019) 4 SCC 17, particularly
paragraph 98, which followed the principle laid down in ArcelorMittal India Pvt.
Ltd. v. Satish Kumar Gupta, (2019) 2 SCC 1, that a resolution applicant,
including a promoter, has no vested right to have its proposal considered
irrespective of compliance with the applicable requirements.
42.
The Respondent No. 1 contended that Section 240A of the Code did not
confer a blanket exemption from the eligibility criteria independently prescribed
under Section 25(2)(h) of the Code, read with Regulation 36A of the IBBI

[PAGE 17]
Page 17 of 41

(Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016
(“CIRP Regulations”). It is submitted that Section 29A prescribes the categories
of persons ineligible to submit a resolution plan, whereas Section 25(2)(h)
empowers the Resolution Professional, with the approval of the CoC, to prescribe
positive eligibility criteria for prospective resolution applicants. According to
Respondent No. 1, Section 240A provides an exemption only in respect of the
disqualifications specified under Sections 29A(c) and 29A(h), and does not
dispense with the separate financial eligibility requirements prescribed under
Section 25(2)(h). The two provisions operate in distinct spheres, and the
exemption under Section 240A cannot be extended to nullify the eligibility
criteria approved by the CoC.
43.
The Respondent No. 1 further submitted that the Appellant had not
contended that he was disqualified under Section 29A(c) or Section 29A(h) of the
Code. Consequently, the exemption under Section 240A, which is specifically
confined to those provisions, was not attracted to the issue arising in the present
Appeal. The Respondent No. 1 contended that the Appellant was, in effect,
seeking an extension of the statutory exemption to dispense with the eligibility
criteria altogether, although no such exemption was available under the Code.
44.

50.
The Respondent No. 1 disputed the Appellant’s reliance upon R.
Raghavendran v. C. Raja John & Ors., arising out of Company Appeal (AT)
(CH) (INS) No. 207 of 2021, and contended that the Appellant’s interpretation of
the decision was incorrect. It is submitted that paragraphs 32 and 34 of the
NCLAT judgment in C. Raja John v. R. Raghavendran, which contained

Respondent No. 1, the Supreme Court had rejected the proposition that, merely
because the Corporate Debtor was an MSME, there was no need to invite or
consider other proposals. Respondent No. 1 therefore contended that the
Adjudicating Authority had not interpreted the Supreme Court’s decision in a
piecemeal manner, as alleged by the Appellant.

62.
After going through the pleading of all parties noted earlier, the following
points arise for decision.
1. Whether Section 240A of the Code exempts the promoter of an MSME
Corporate Debtor from the net worth criterion fixed by the CoC under
Section 25(2)(h) in the present case. Whether the word invite in Section
25(2)(h) means that the criteria apply only to outsiders and not to the
promoter. Whether, in the alternative, any exceptional circumstance is
made out for the appellant.
2. Whether the criterion of Rs. 400 crores, as applied to all applicants
including the appellant, is arbitrary or beyond the CoC’s commercial

[PAGE 27]
Page 27 of 41

wisdom, and whether it was wrong to reject the EOI of the Appellant
without placing it before the CoC.
3. Whether the Claim Order, which directs re-computation of the claim,
makes the criterion irrational or calls for a fresh Form G.
4. Whether any case of mala fide or bias against the RP or the CoC is made
out.
63.
At this stage, we state that all these issues are interconnected,
interdependent and are required to be examined in wholistic manner and we shall
examine all these issues in conjoint manner in subsequent part of our judgement,
treating all issues as single point for determination, for reaching outcome of the
present appeal.
64.
At the outset, it needs to be appreciated that the Section 240A(1) of the
Code stipulates that, despite anything contrary in the Code, clauses (c) and (h) of
Section 29A shall not apply to a resolution applicant in the CIRP of an MSME.
Clause (c) is the bar on a person whose account, or the account of a corporate
debtor under his management or control, is classified as a non-performing asset.
Clause (h) is the bar on a person who has given a guarantee for a corporate debtor
whose guarantee has been invoked and remains unpaid. The sub-section also
applies to a pre-packaged insolvency resolution process. These two bars were
removed for MSMEs because, as the Insolvency Law Committee noted, the
business of an MSME attracts interest primarily from its own promoter and may
not attract other resolution applicants.

[PAGE 28]
Page 28 of 41

65.
We also observe that Section 240A (2) allows the Central Government, in
public interest, to notify that any provision of the Code shall not apply to MSMEs,
or shall apply with exceptions, modifications and adaptations, however, we find
that no such notification has been issued and the appellant has also not pointed to
any. We have also not come across any notification that relaxes the criteria that a
CoC may fix under Section 25(2)(h). The exemption is therefore narrow and
specific. Parliament has named the two clauses of Section 29A that do not apply
in the present case. It has not named Section 25(2)(h), nor any other provision on
eligibility. We are of the opinion that neither the Adjudicating Authority nor this
Appellate Tribunal can add to a list that the legislature has made so precisely and
any attempt to do so would be to rewrite the law.
66.
We further observe that Section 29A and Section 25(2)(h) work at different
levels. Section 29A is a list of persons who are disqualified from submitting a
plan. It is a negative test, and it applies to everyone. Section 25(2)(h) is a positive
test. It lets the RP, with the approval of the CoC, set criteria that applicants must
meet, having regard to the complexity and scale of operations of the business of
the corporate debtor. A person who is not disqualified under Section 29A, or who
is freed from clauses (c) and (h) by Section 240A, has only cleared the first gate.
He must still meet the criteria for that particular resolution process.
67.
The appellant’s case, in the end, is not that Section 240A takes away the
net worth criterion. In his own pleadings, the Appellant stated that he did not
claim an automatic right to be approved or to be freed from competition. It is the

[PAGE 29]
Page 29 of 41

case of the Appellant that the criterion cannot be applied as an absolute and
mechanical bar. Thus, we find that the case of the Appellant rests on the ground
of exceptional circumstances, and we deal with it below. On the plain words of
the Code, the Adjudicating Authority was right that Section 240A (1) is limited
to clauses (c) and (h) of Section 29A and that MSME status by itself does not
exempt a promoter from the net worth criterion.
68.
The appellant also argued that Section 25(2)(h) cannot be used to defeat
the object of the code which grants exemption to MSME in order to try to revive
itself. However, we are not in position to agree with this way of putting it. We are
of the opinion that the object of Section 240A is to keep the MSME promoter
from being shut out by Section 29A(c) and (h). The promoter has not been shut
out on that ground. No one has said that the Appellant is disqualified under
Section 29A. The question is only whether he meets a criterion that applies to all.
A criterion on financial strength, applied equally to everyone, does not defeat the
object of Section 240A.
69.
The Appellant argued that Section 25(2)(h) is addressed to outsiders. He
wanted us to accept his logic that the RP invites the outsider through EOI based
on eligibility criteria and a promoter does not need any invitation to participate
and meet these requirements. We cannot accept this for these reasons. First, we
observe that the Code has no separate class of promoter-applicants. Section 5(25)
defines a “resolution applicant” as a person who, alone or jointly with others,
submits a resolution plan to the RP pursuant to the invitation made under clause

[PAGE 30]
Page 30 of 41

(h) of sub-section (2) of Section 25. A promoter who wants to submit a plan is,
by definition, such a person. He gets in through the same invitation as everyone
else.
70.
Second, Section 29A itself shows that promoters are treated as applicants.
It stipulates that a person who is not eligible shall not submit a plan, and many of
its clauses are aimed at the promoter and those connected with him. The Code
allows the promoter to come back, but only as a resolution applicant and under
the same process. Section 240A (1) also speaks of the resolution applicant in an
MSME, which confirms the point.
71.
Third, the criteria under Section 25(2)(h) exist so that the CoC is shown a
plan from a person who can carry it out. That concern does not go away because
the applicant is the promoter of the MSME Corporate Debtor. If anything, the
CoC may take greater care when the person who ran the company into default
offers to take it back. The statute makes no exception for him.
72.
The appellant argued that, even if there is no automatic right, the many
facts together are exceptional: (i) the Corporate Debtor is an MSME; (ii) he is the
original promoter; (iii) he filed his EOI in time and deposited Rs. 5 crores; (iv)
he filed his income tax returns and credit record, and gave an undertaking to prove
the source of funds; (v) he claims to have brought about the repayment of about
Rs. 182 crores to the creditor; (vi) the EOI was rejected at the threshold; and (vii)
the claim has to be re-computed.

[PAGE 31]
Page 31 of 41

73.
We have examined each of these. MSME status and being the original
promoter are the very facts that Section 240A already takes into account. They
are the starting point of any such case. They cannot be exceptional circumstances,
because they are present in every MSME promoter’s case. If they were enough,
the principle in Saravana Global Holdings Ltd. & Anr. v. Bafna Pharmaceuticals
Ltd. & Ors., 2019 SCC OnLine NCLAT 962, which the Supreme Court has
rejected as a general rule, would return by another route.
74.
The timely filing of the EOI, and the earnest money, show that the appellant
is serious. They do not show that he is able to pay. The criterion is about financial
capacity, not about seriousness. The appellant’s income tax returns for AY 2023-
24, 2024-25 and 2025-26 and his credit score of 776 are also not a substitute for
it. A return shows income for a year. A credit score shows how a person has
repaid his borrowings. Neither shows the net worth of a person, which is the value
of his assets after liabilities. The appellant has not placed before us any statement
of his net worth, even in a form that a Chartered Accountant could certify. His
case has been that he need not furnish one.
75.
We also observe that an undertaking to prove source of funds later does not
meet the criterion either. It is a promise to be proved at a future time. The CoC
fixed a net worth floor so that the capacity is shown at the beginning, before the
process spends time and resources on an applicant and is applicable for all
Resolution Applicant without any exception.

[PAGE 32]
Page 32 of 41

76.
The claim that the appellant brought about repayment of about Rs. 182
crore stands on the appellant’s own account. It relates to a Debenture Trust Deed
dated 24.03.2022, which is different from the Debenture Trust Deed of
27.03.2018 and has not been tested in these proceedings. Whatever the truth, it
does not tell us anything about his net worth today. It cannot be treated as proof
of capacity. The fact that the EOI was rejected at the threshold, and the effect of
the Claim Order, on their own do not make the case to be treated as exceptional.
77.
The appellant also pleaded that the value of the Corporate Debtor was built
up by him before the CIRP, and that his shares are now of no value, so his net
worth is directly tied to the Corporate Debtor. This is correct to some extent. But
a promoter’s net worth is not limited to his shares in the Corporate Debtor. It
takes in all his assets. The appellant has not pleaded that, outside the Corporate
Debtor, he has a net worth of Rs. 400 crore or anything near it. He has also not
said that he was unable to obtain a certificate. The Code expects a promoter who
has led his company into insolvency to show that he can now bring in the money.
The fall in the value of his shares is the result of the default and the CIRP. It
cannot be a reason to relax the test.
78.
We therefore find that the appellant has not shown any exceptional
circumstance, even if the Saravana principle (supra) were taken as still available
in a case like this.
79.
We also observe that Section 25(2)(h) gives the power to fix criteria to the
RP, but only with the approval of the CoC. It must be exercised having regard to

[PAGE 33]
Page 33 of 41

the complexity and scale of the business. In this case, the CoC approved the
criteria at its second meeting. The Adjudicating Authority recorded that the net
worth was fixed at Rs. 400 crores because the Corporate Debtor owns a large land
parcel with development potential above Rs. 5,000 crores, and about 10 lakh sq.
ft. of area. The appellant himself submitted that the value of the Corporate
Debtor’s assets is about Rs. 5,000 crores. So, there is no dispute that the business
is large and valuable. Seen in this light, the floor of Rs. 400 crores are 8% of the
asset value that the appellant himself relies on. It is about 17% of the claim of Rs.
2,312.68 crore that Respondent No. 2 has filed. We find it to be a reasoned and
proportionate figure. We are of clear opinion that a person who cannot show that
level of financial strength may not be able to complete a project of this size. The
CoC was entitled to take that view.
80.
We may also mention that it is by now a well settled legal position that the
scope of review of the CoC’s commercial wisdom is limited. The Supreme Court
held in K. Sashidhar v. Indian Overseas Bank, 2019 SCCOnLine SC 257 and in
Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel India Ltd. v. Satish Kumar Gupta, [2019]
16 SCR 275 that the commercial wisdom of the CoC is not open to judicial review,
except within the narrow limits the Code itself provides. We are of the considered
view that a criterion on eligibility is a business decision about who the creditors
are willing to deal with. The Appellant has not shown that it breaks any provision
of the Code, or that it is discriminatory or plainly arbitrary. It applies to every

[PAGE 34]
Page 34 of 41

category of applicant. One applicant was admitted after producing the documents,
and none was given a relaxation. We find this is the opposite of discrimination.
81.
The appellant also argued that the CoC is a single creditor with whom he
has long-standing disputes on the Debenture Trust Deed of 27.03.2018. We are
of view that even for arguments sake it may be true; however, a single financial
creditor forms the CoC when it is the only one. Its voting share is the full share.
Disputes between a creditor and a promoter are common in insolvency and, by
themselves, do not make the creditor’s business decisions suspect. The claim
itself is being tested in the proper proceedings, and the Adjudicating Authority
has already corrected the method of computing interest. That shows that the
appellant has an effective remedy for his real grievance on the claim, and that the
Adjudicating Authority has acted on it.
82.
The appellant’s argument that the criterion was framed to keep him out
also does not stand. It was approved on 04.05.2026 and applies, in the same
words, to every class of applicant. There is nothing on record to show that it was
drafted with him in mind. The RP wrote to the Appellant on 10.06.2026 and gave
him a chance to explain. The Appellant replied on 18.06.2026 and his EOI was
rejected only after that. This shows that the Appellant was heard before the
decision.
83.
The appellant has submitted that it is unfair to ask a promoter of an MSME
to show a net worth that is likely to have been affected by the insolvency itself.
We have kept this in mind. The CoC is free to relax a criterion, if it so decides,

[PAGE 35]
Page 35 of 41

for a promoter whose case it finds deserving. The Code leaves that decision to the
CoC, as the body that bears the commercial risk. It does not leave it to this
Appellate Tribunal. The CoC was requested of the issue and declined to give a
waiver.
84.
The appellant argues that the RP rejected the EOI at the threshold, so the
CoC never applied its commercial wisdom to his proposal. We do not agree for
three reasons.
85.
First, the scheme of Regulation 36A of the CIRP Regulations is that the RP
examines each EOI against the criteria the CoC has already approved. Regulation
36A(7) requires an EOI to be unconditional and to carry the undertakings and
records that show the applicant meets the CoC’s criteria and is not barred by
Section 29A. Regulation 36A(8) requires the RP to carry out due diligence, on
the basis of the material on record, to check that the EOI meets the requirements
of Section 25(2)(h), Section 29A and the invitation. Provisional and final lists are
then published, with a chance to object (Regulation 36A(10) to (12)). The CoC
does not have to consider each EOI one by one at that stage. Its commercial
wisdom is exercised when it lays down the criteria and later when it votes on a
plan. The RP applied the criterion that the CoC had set. Doing so was his duty.
86.
Second, the appellant’s EOI did not meet Regulation 36A (7). It carried the
standard undertaking that the applicant meets the CoC’s criteria, while the net
worth item was struck out as “not applicable”. An EOI that strikes out a term of

[PAGE 36]
Page 36 of 41

the invitation is not unconditional. The RP did not reject it at once. He wrote to
the Appellant to give him a chance to comply, and the Appellant chose not to.
87.
Third, the record shows that the CoC did consider the case of the Appellant.
As the RP has stated, the third meeting (17.06.2026) took up the EOIs under
verification, and the fourth meeting (20.07.2026) recorded that no waiver can be
given to anyone at the EOI stage. If this is so, the CoC has in fact applied its mind
to the question of relaxation, though it did so after the rejection and not before it.
The appellant argues that he had no vote in the meetings. That is correct and
makes no difference. The question was decided by the only body that has the
power to decide it.
88.
Now we will deal with the argument of the Appellant based on the Chennai
Bench judgement. We note that the Chennai Bench of this Appellate Tribunal in
C. Raja John v. R. Raghavendra (supra) followed Saravana (supra) and allowed
an MSME promoter to file a net worth certificate. The Hon’ble Supreme Court,
in R. Raghavendran v. C. Raja John, 2023 INSC 849, held that this broad
reasoning, that an MSME promoter need not compete with others, is not the
correct position of law, and it set aside paragraphs 32 and 34 of the Chennai
judgment which reads as under: -
“32) In any event, it is unequivocal that the Corporate Debtor is
an MSME and as held by this Tribunal that it is not necessary for
the Promoters to compete with other Resolution Applicants to
regain the control of the Corporate Debtor.

[PAGE 37]
Page 37 of 41

34) Further, this Tribunal, keeping in view of the object of the
Code that the Maximization of the Value of the Assets of Corporate
Debtor is to be kept in mind in achieving its object. To give an
opportunity to regain the control of the Corporate Debtor, the
Management/Promoters/Erstwhile Directors of the Corporate
Debtor being an MSME, not necessary to compete with other
Resolution Applicants.”
89.
The Hon’ble Supreme Court explained that Saravana (supra) was decided
in a different setting, either before the CoC was constituted or in a Section 12A
withdrawal. The appellant says the Hon’ble Supreme Court only clarified the law
and that the other paragraphs of the Chennai judgment (supra) remain. We do
not agree, because once the broad reasoning is set aside, an MSME promoter
cannot claim, as a general rule, to stay out of the competitive process. Saravana
(supra) can apply only in the narrow settings the Hon’ble Supreme Court
described, and this CIRP is neither of them. The direction to consider the
promoter's plan in Raghavendran (supra) was tied to its own facts, where the net
worth floor was only Rs. 2 crores, a plan had already been filed, and the defect
could be cured by a certificate. Here the floor is Rs. 400 crores, only an EOI has
been filed, and the appellant refused to file any certificate. The Adjudicating
Authority was therefore right in holding that C. Raja John (supra), as it stands
after Raghavendran (supra), does not help the appellant. We also find no fault in
the way it read the Chennai judgment (supra), as it looked at what the Hon’ble

[PAGE 38]
Page 38 of 41

Supreme Court set aside and what it said about Saravana (supra), which is the
correct way to read it.
90.
We make it clear that we are not holding that a CoC can never relax the net
worth criterion for an MSME promoter. A CoC may do so, if it thinks fit in the
interest of a better resolution. All we hold is that no tribunal can compel it to do
so on the basis of MSME status.
91.
The appellant relied on Regulation 36A(4) of the CIRP Regulations for the
argument that it deals with the CoC’s power to relax criteria and is not an
exhaustive code on classes entitled to different treatment. Even on this reading,
the power to relax is that of the CoC and not of the RP or Adjudicating Authority
or even this Appellate Tribunal. The CoC has declined to use it. We have not been
shown any provision in Regulation 36A that exempts an MSME promoter from
the criteria. The absence of such an exemption in the Regulation does not, as the
Appellant argues, leave Section 240A to fill the gap, because Section 240A is
limited as we have held.
92.
The Appellant argued that the aim of the net worth criterion could have
been met in other ways, such as proof of funds, funding commitments, comfort
letters and undertakings. This was a matter for the CoC. It chose the net worth
certificate as the test, because it is an independent and verifiable document. A
tribunal cannot replace it with another test of its own choosing. The CoC is not
barred from accepting other forms of comfort, but it is not bound to.

[PAGE 39]
Page 39 of 41

93.
The Appellant argued that, because the claim of Respondent No. 2 is to be
re-computed, the financial parameters have changed. In his view, a revised
Information Memorandum and a fresh Form G must be issued, the floor of Rs.
400 crores have lost its basis, and this is a further exceptional circumstance. We
do not accept this for the following reasons.
A) The floor was fixed with reference to the size and value of the business, in
particular the land. It was not fixed as a percentage of the claim. The Claim
Order does not change the value of the land or the scale of the project. The
appellant himself submitted the assets are worth about Rs. 5,000 crores.
Even if the claim falls below Rs. 1,000 crores, as the appellant expects, Rs.
400 crores may still be considered a reasonable fraction of what is at stake.
B) The revised figure is not yet known. The statement that it will be below Rs.
1,000 crores are the appellant’s projection. The Claim Order only sets the
method of computing interest and directs the RP to re-compute on that
basis, after the creditor has supplied details of disbursements and charges.
The result is not before us.
C) The Claim Order is under challenge in Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency)
No. 1809 of 2026. We say nothing on its merits, till it is decided by us.
D) Whether the Information Memorandum needs to be revised, and whether
Form G needs to be published again, are matters for the RP and the CoC
under Regulation 36(2)(d) and Regulation 36A of the CIRP Regulations.
The legality of the rejection dated 22.06.2026 must be judged on the facts

[PAGE 40]
Page 40 of 41

and the criteria as they stood on that date. A later event cannot make a
lawful rejection unlawful.
94.
We are of the opinion that if a fresh Form G is issued by the RP, the criteria
for it will be decided by the CoC at that time. The Appellant, like any other
resolution applicant, will be free to respond to that invitation, subject to the
criteria then in force. Our findings do not stand in his way in that event.
95.
The Appellant has submitted that the criteria were framed in a mala fide
and vexatious manner to keep him out, and that the RP is biased and prejudiced.
These are serious allegations. They need a clear basis in facts. We find none.
96.
As to the criterion, we have already held that it was approved by the CoC,
applies to all, and is proportionate to the size of the business. As to the RP, the
record shows that he followed the CoC’s criteria. He applied it equally to the three
EOIs that were short of documents, and he wrote to the Appellant to give him a
chance to explain. He refunded the earnest money after the rejection. A resolution
professional who applies the CoC’s criteria as they stand is performing his duty
and is not acting with bias. The Appellant’s displeasure with the outcome is not
evidence of bias. We therefore reject these allegations.
97.
The Appellant has also pleaded that the Rs. 182 crore that he claims to have
repaid, and the disputes on the Debenture Trust Deed of 27.03.2018, show that
the creditor has been unfair to him. These matters belong to the proceedings on
the claim and on the debt. They do not form any basis for exempting him from a
criterion that applies to every applicant.

[PAGE 41]
Page 41 of 41

98.
The Adjudicating Authority held that Section 240A(1) covers only clauses
(c) and (h) of Section 29A, that the CoC acted within its power under Section
25(2)(h) and its commercial wisdom, and that MSME status alone does not take
away the net worth requirement. For the reasons given above, we agree with these
conclusions.
