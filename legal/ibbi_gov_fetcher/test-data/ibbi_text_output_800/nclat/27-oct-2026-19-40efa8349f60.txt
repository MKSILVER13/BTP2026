These two Appeals arise out of the same order dated 25.07.2025
passed by the Ld. Adjudicating Authority (National Company Law Tribunal,
Mumbai), in I.A. No. 6304/2024 in C.P. (IB) 450/(PB)/2018. The First
appeal, Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 has been preferred by M/S
VASU BUILDCON/Appellant and the Second appeal, Comp. App. (AT)
(Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025 has been filed by Mr. Ankit Pathak/Suspended
Director of IVR Prime IT SEZ Pvt. Ltd. (Corporate Debtor). Both the
parties are aggrieved by the impugned order passed by Ld. Adjudicating
Authority in I.A. No. 6304/2024 and have assailed the same before this
Appellate Tribunal.

Analysis and Findings

84.
The principal question arising for consideration in the present
Appeal is whether the Adjudicating Authority was justified in declining to
interfere with the decision of the CoC to permit publication of the Third
Form-G for inviting fresh Expressions of Interest in the CIRP of the
Corporate Debtor.

[PAGE 34]
Page 34 of 55
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025
85.
The Appeal arises from the order dated 25.07.2025 passed in IA No.
6304/2024, whereby the Adjudicating Authority permitted the Resolution
Professional to proceed with publication of the Third Form-G. The said
application itself arose from the resolution passed by the CoC in its 25th
Meeting held on 04.12.2024. Thus, though several issues have been raised
by the Appellants concerning the earlier Resolution Plan, the Section 12A
proposal, the constitution of the CoC and subsequent assignments, the
immediate question before us is whether, in the circumstances existing at
the relevant time, the CoC could take a decision to invite fresh Expressions
of Interest and whether the Adjudicating Authority was justified in
declining to interfere with that decision.
86.
The CIRP of the Corporate Debtor commenced on 19.09.2019. In the
course of the CIRP, the Resolution Plan submitted by M/s Vasu Buildcon
was approved by the CoC with 100% voting share in its 16th Meeting held
on 31.03.2022. Pursuant thereto, IA No. 2878/2022 was filed before the
Adjudicating Authority under Sections 30(6) and 31 of the Code seeking
approval of the Resolution Plan. The original Resolution Plan contemplated
a resolution value of approximately Rs.41.63 Crores. It is therefore not in
dispute that, at that stage, the Plan had received the requisite approval of
the CoC. The difficulty, however, is that the approval of the CoC did not
result in approval of the Plan under Section 31 of the Code, as the
application for approval remained pending before the Adjudicating
Authority.

[PAGE 35]
Page 35 of 55
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025
87.
While the aforesaid application was pending, the Supreme Court
delivered its judgment in Greater Noida Industrial Development Authority v.
Prabhjit Singh Soni, 2024 SCC OnLine SC 122. In view of the legal position
emerging from the said judgment, the Adjudicating Authority, by order
dated 07.06.2024, remitted the Resolution Plan to the CoC for
reconsideration. The aforesaid order is extracted below:

[PAGE 36]
Page 36 of 55
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025

88.
The effect of the said order is significant. The original Plan,
notwithstanding its approval with 100% voting share, had not attained the
status of a Resolution Plan approved under Section 31 of the Code and was
specifically remitted for reconsideration. The CoC was therefore required to

[PAGE 37]
Page 37 of 55
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025
reconsider the proposal in the light of the order of remand and the legal
position applicable at that stage. The subsequent proceedings cannot
consequently be examined on the assumption that the approval granted in
the 16th CoC Meeting continued to operate unaffected by the order of
remand.
89.
In our view, the Appellant cannot derive an absolute right to insist
upon approval of the original Resolution Plan merely from the fact that the
same had earlier received 100% voting share. The distinction between
approval by the CoC under Section 30(4) and approval by the Adjudicating
Authority under Section 31 is material in the facts of the present case.
Since the Plan had not been approved under Section 31 and had
subsequently been remitted for reconsideration, the position which existed
on 31.03.2022 could not be treated as final and incapable of further
consideration. The order dated 07.06.2024 reopened the consideration of
the Plan before the CoC, and it is the consequence of that reconsideration
which has to be examined for determining the validity of the subsequent
decision to issue the Third Form-G.
90.
Pursuant to the order dated 07.06.2024, M/s Vasu Buildcon
submitted a revised Resolution Plan dated 24.10.2024, which was placed
before the CoC in its 24th Meeting held on 30.10.2024. The revised Plan
contemplated a substantially enhanced resolution value of approximately
Rs.228.26 Crores as against approximately Rs.41.63 Crores under the
original Plan. It also contemplated payment of approximately Rs.196.63
Crores to the Greater Noida Industrial Development Authority over a period

[PAGE 38]
Page 38 of 55
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025
of 26 months. The revised proposal thus involved a substantial increase in
the financial commitments contemplated under the earlier Plan. The CoC
was consequently required to consider not merely the existence of the
revised proposal, but also its feasibility and viability and the capacity of
the Resolution Applicant to implement the obligations undertaken therein.
91.
The minutes of the 24th CoC Meeting record several concerns in
relation to the revised Plan. The funding mechanism was not considered
sufficiently clear; the commitment letters from Canara Bank were
conditional and subject to fresh due diligence; the Performance Guarantee
of Rs.28 Crores had not been furnished despite the stipulated deadline of
28.10.2024; and no updated net worth certificate or audited balance sheet
was placed before the CoC to demonstrate the financial capacity to
implement a Plan valued at approximately Rs.228.26 Crores. The CoC also
considered the issue of the experience and capacity of M/s Vasu Buildcon
to execute a project of the scale involved, covering approximately 1,88,176
square metres. The record further notes the absence of M/s Vasu Buildcon
from the 24th CoC Meeting despite notice and the provision of the video-
conferencing link. These were not merely formal objections but matters
which went to the feasibility and viability of the revised proposal.
92.
However, notwithstanding non-compliance with the conditions of the
RFRP the CoC took into consideration the revised plan of Appellant and it
was placed for voting. The voting concluded on 25.11.2024. The CoC, for
the relevant period, comprised the Financial Creditors in the class of
allottees through their Authorised Representative having 34.69% voting

[PAGE 39]
Page 39 of 55
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025
share, Nupur Finvest Pvt. Ltd. having 8.61% voting share and ICICI Bank
having 56.70% voting share. The revised Plan of Appellant received 0% vote
in favour and 43.30% votes were recorded against it. The ICICI Bank,
having 56.70% voting share, abstained from voting, while the statutory
requirement under Section 30(4) was 66%. In effect, the plan was rejected
with 0% votes in favour of the plan. The revised Resolution Plan therefore
failed to secure the requisite majority.
93.
On consideration of the above, we note that the revised Resolution
Plan was not rejected without consideration. The material placed before us
shows that the CoC examined the revised proposal with reference to its
financial structure, source of funds, Performance Guarantee, financial
capacity and ability to implement the substantially enhanced obligations.
Ultimately, after the Plan was placed to vote, it received “Nil” support from
the CoC. For approval of the plan 66% of voting share of CoC is statutorily
required. Whether the commercial assessment of the CoC was the most
appropriate assessment is not a question which can be substituted by this
Tribunal in view of the failure of Resolution Plan the next the statutory
threshold the approval of CoC as prescribed in Code.
94.
We further note that the rejection of the revised Plan was not
independently challenged by the Appellant before the Adjudicating
Authority. In these circumstances, the failure of the revised Plan to secure
the requisite voting share constitutes an intervening and material fact,
which cannot be indirectly reopened in an Appeal directed against the
subsequent decision to issue the Third Form-G.

95.
After the revised Resolution Plan failed to secure the statutory
majority, the CoC proceeded to consider the other course available for
bringing the CIRP to an end. A proposal under Section 12A filed by ex-
Directors of Corporate Debtor had earlier been kept in abeyance in the 23rd
CoC Meeting held on 21.10.2024 pending consideration of the revised
Resolution Plan. The proposal was thereafter taken up in the 25th CoC
Meeting held on 04.12.2024. Upon voting, the Section 12A proposal was
rejected with 84.86% votes against it, whereas a 90% voting share was
required for approval. Thus, the settlement proposal also failed to secure
the statutory threshold prescribed under the Code.
96.
The sequence in which the Section 12A proposal was considered is
relevant. The CoC did not proceed to initiate a fresh market process
immediately upon rejection of the revised Resolution Plan. It also
considered whether the CIRP could instead be ended through settlement
under Section 12A. That proposal was subjected to the prescribed voting
process but instead of receiving requisite 90% votes in favour, it received
84.86% against the proposal. We therefore find that the CoC had
considered the principal alternative to continuation of the resolution
process and that alternative had also failed. The decision of CoC which
followed thereafter has consequently to be examined in the context of both
these developments.

99.
It is against the above background that the decision taken in the
25th CoC Meeting dated 04.12.2024 assumes significance. In the very
same meeting in which the Section 12A proposal was considered and

[PAGE 42]
Page 42 of 55
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and

rejected, the CoC resolved to publish the Third Form-G inviting fresh
Expressions of Interest from Prospective Resolution Applicants. The
resolution was passed with 100% voting share. IA No. 6304/2024 was
thereafter filed by the Resolution Professional seeking permission of the
Adjudicating Authority to publish the fresh Form-G. The impugned order
dated 25.07.2025 was passed in the said IA. The record therefore shows a
clear sequence: the original Plan had been remitted; the revised Plan had
failed to obtain the requisite majority; the Section 12A proposal filed by the
Suspended Director had also failed to pass muster with CoC; and
thereafter the CoC unanimously decided to explore a fresh resolution
process, as it did not have any other option except liquidation, which is
always the last resort in CIRP proceedings.
100. The Appellant's principal objection is that the original Resolution
Plan had already been approved with 100% voting share and that there
was no justification for abandoning the same and initiating a fresh market
process. The Appellant also relies upon the order dated 07.06.2024, the
time-bound nature of the CIRP and the provisions of the CIRP Regulations
to contend that the CoC could not, in effect, reopen the resolution process.
These submissions, however, proceed on the assumption that the original
Plan continued to remain available for approval in the same form even after
the order of remand and the subsequent reconsideration. That assumption
does not hold any ground as the original plan of the Appellant had been
remitted for reconsideration; based upon which, the appellant had
submitted a revised Plan; that revised Plan was considered by the CoC and
it was rejected by 100% voting share of the CoC. Also, the original

[PAGE 43]
Page 43 of 55
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025
resolution plan of the appellant had never received approval under Section
31.
101. In our view, therefore, the decision to issue the Third Form-G cannot
be characterised as an arbitrary abandonment of an approved Resolution
Plan. The CoC was not dealing with a Resolution Plan which had already
received approval under Section 31 and had thereafter been sought to be
displaced. It was dealing with a Plan which had been remitted for
reconsideration in accordance with a judgment of Hon’ble Supreme Court
in Prabhjot Soni (supra). The revised plan submitted by the appellant in
compliance with the order of the Ld. Adjudicating Authority had
subsequently failed to obtain the statutory majority. The distinction is
fundamental. Once the revised proposal failed to secure the voting
threshold prescribed under Section 30(4), the earlier approval of the
Resolution Plan by CoC has been overturned by subsequent action of the
CoC. The original plan was no longer available for subsequent
consideration of the CoC. We are therefore of the view that the order dated
07.06.2024 did not place any condition on the CoC's for deciding the
further course of the resolution process.
102. The Appellant has also relied upon Regulation 36A and other
provisions of the CIRP Regulations to contend that a fresh Form-G could
not have been issued in the circumstances of the present case. Regulation
36A(4A) provides that any modification in the invitation for expression of
interest may be made in the manner in which the initial invitation was
made, subject to the limitation contained therein. The provision, however,

[PAGE 44]
Page 44 of 55
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025
does not establish that, in every situation where a resolution proposal
considered by the CoC fails to secure the statutory majority, the CoC is
permanently prohibited from inviting fresh Expressions of Interest. The
question before us is not whether Regulation 36A independently confers
an unrestricted power to issue a fresh Form-G, but whether the decision
actually taken by the CoC is prohibited by the Code or the Regulations in
the circumstances of the present case.
103. We notice no such statutory prohibition in the relevant regulation.
The present case is materially different from a situation where a Resolution
Plan has already been approved by the Adjudicating Authority under
Section 31 and a fresh process is sought to be commenced merely because
the CoC subsequently changes its commercial preference. Here, the
original Plan had not been approved under Section 31, the matter had been
remitted by the Adjudicating Authority, the revised Plan had failed to
obtain the requisite majority, and the Section 12A proposal had also failed.
In such circumstances, the CoC was required to determine the further
course of the CIRP. The fact that the Code requires the CIRP to be
conducted within a time-bound framework cannot be interpreted to mean
that the CoC must proceed with a proposal which has failed to obtain the
statutory approval required under Section 30(4).
104. The decision in Ashdan Properties Pvt. Ltd. v. Mamta Binani (RP of
Rolta India Ltd.) & Ors., 2024 SCC OnLine NCLAT 1652, also supports the
proposition that, in appropriate circumstances, the CoC may decide not to
proceed with an existing resolution process and may take steps for inviting

[PAGE 45]
Page 45 of 55
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025
fresh Expressions of Interest. We do not read the said decision as laying
down that a fresh Form-G can be issued as a matter of course in every
CIRP. Its relevance is that the existence of an earlier invitation or earlier
resolution process does not, by itself, create an absolute legal bar against
a subsequent fresh market process when the circumstances otherwise
justify such a course. In the present case, the fresh Form-G followed the
failure of the revised Plan and the Section 12A proposal and was approved
unanimously by the CoC.
105. We therefore find that the Appellant has not established any
statutory prohibition which rendered the decision of the CoC to issue the
Third Form-G impermissible. The impugned order also cannot be faulted
merely because the Adjudicating Authority did not accept the Appellant's
interpretation of the order dated 07.06.2024. The said order required
reconsideration of the Resolution Plan; it did not mandate approval of the
revised proposal or require the CoC to continue indefinitely with the same
Resolution Applicant notwithstanding the failure of the revised Plan to
obtain the statutory majority.
106. Having reached the above conclusion, the next question is whether
the decision of the CoC to issue the Third Form-G calls for interference on
the ground that the CoC acted beyond the permissible limits of its
commercial wisdom. The answer has to be considered from the sequence
of decisions actually taken by the CoC. The revised Resolution Plan was
considered in the 24th Meeting and was subjected to voting. It failed to
obtain the requisite 66% majority. Thereafter, the Section 12A proposal

[PAGE 46]
Page 46 of 55
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025
was considered in the 25th Meeting and failed to obtain the requisite 90%
majority. It was only thereafter that the CoC unanimously resolved, with
100% voting share, to invite fresh Expressions of Interest. Thus, the
decision to issue the Third Form-G was not taken in isolation but followed
consideration of the alternatives available with CoC in a sequential and
logical manner.
107. The commercial wisdom of the CoC is, of course, subject to the
requirements of the IBC and the applicable Regulations, and judicial review
is available where a decision is contrary to law or suffers from a legally
recognised infirmity. At the same time, where the CoC has considered a
Resolution Plan, assessed its feasibility and viability, voted upon it in
accordance with the statutory threshold, considered a Section 12A
proposal and thereafter unanimously chosen to invite fresh Expressions of
Interest, the Tribunal cannot substitute its own commercial assessment
for that of the CoC merely because another course may also have been
possible. The record in the present case does not disclose that the CoC
proceeded without considering the revised Plan or acted in disregard of the
statutory process.
108. The concerns recorded in the 24th CoC Meeting assume relevance in
this regard. The CoC had before it a revised Plan involving a resolution
value of approximately Rs.228.26 Crores, substantially higher than the
original Plan value. It examined the funding arrangements, the conditional
nature of the bank commitments, the absence of the Performance
Guarantee, the financial capacity of the Resolution Applicant and its

[PAGE 47]
Page 47 of 55
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025
implementation capability. The Plan was nevertheless placed for voting and
could not secure a single vote of CoC in its favour. Its failure to secure the
requisite majority was therefore the outcome of the statutory voting process
after consideration of the proposal. We find no basis to conclude that the
subsequent decision to seek fresh Expressions of Interest was arbitrary or
that the Adjudicating Authority erred in declining to interfere with that
decision.
109. Accordingly, on the principal issue arising from IA No. 6304/2024,
we are of the view that the CoC was entitled, in the circumstances of the
present case, to initiate a fresh market process after the revised Resolution
Plan failed to obtain the statutory majority and the Section 12A proposal
was also rejected. The Adjudicating Authority, while passing the impugned
order dated 25.07.2025, correctly examined the effect of the remand order,
the subsequent consideration of the revised Plan and the decision of the
CoC and declined to substitute its own assessment for the decision taken
by the CoC. No ground has been made out warranting interference with
the impugned order.
110. The Appellant has relied upon Ebix Singapore Pvt. Ltd. v.
Committee of Creditors of Educomp Solutions Ltd., (2022) 2 SCC 401,
wherein Hon’ble Supreme Court considered whether a successful
Resolution Applicant could withdraw or modify a Resolution Plan after its
approval by the CoC, but before approval by the Adjudicating Authority
under Section 31. The Court held that, the resolution plan once approved
by the CoC and submitted to the Adjudicating Authority becomes binding

CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025
and cannot be withdrawn or modified at the instance of the successful

introduce
uncertainty
and
further
negotiations
into
the
CIRP.
In the present case, however, the Appellant’s original Plan, though
approved with 100% voting share on 31.03.2022, was not approved under
Section 31 and was subsequently remitted to the CoC by order dated
07.06.2024 for reconsideration in light of judgment of Hon’ble SC in
Prabhjot Soni (supra). Pursuant thereto, the Appellant itself submitted a
revised Plan dated 24.10.2024, which was considered in the 24th CoC
Meeting but failed to secure the statutory 66% majority, with 43.30%
voting against the Plan and ICICI Bank’s 56.70% not voting in its favour.
The present case does not involve SRA seeking to withdraw or modify a
subsisting CoC-approved Plan, but concerns the consequence of a revised
Plan failing to obtain the requisite statutory majority after judicial remand,
consequently, the ratio in Ebix does not support the present factual matrix.
111. We now turn to the Intervention Applications. In the present case, IA
Nos. 7215 of 2025 and 7783 of 2025 have been preferred by certain
allottees of the real estate project “Aranya”, who are stated to be creditors
in the separate CIRP of Unnati Fortune Holding Ltd., the parent company
of the Corporate Debtor. Since the intervenors were not parties to the
proceedings resulting in the impugned order, the scope of their
participation has necessarily to be considered with reference to the
controversy arising from the said order. The principle governing
intervention in appellate proceedings is that an intervenor may assist the
Court by addressing arguments in support of one or the other existing

[PAGE 49]
Page 49 of 55
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1212 of 2025 and
CA (AT) (Ins.) No. 1225 of 2025
parties but cannot, under the guise of intervention, introduce an entirely
independent cause of action or seek relief beyond the subject matter of the
appeal. The Supreme Court in Saraswati Industrial Syndicate Ltd. v.
Commissioner of Income Tax, Haryana, Rohtak, (1999) 3 SCC 141, has
recognised the limited purpose for which intervention is permitted in
appellate proceedings.
112. The material placed before us indicates that the intervenors seek to
raise issues concerning, inter alia, consolidation of the CIRP of Unnati
Fortune Holding Ltd. with that of the CD and the treatment of their
interests under the Resolution Plan consequent upon publication of the
fresh Form-G. These issues do not arise from the impugned order dated
25.07.2025, which was confined to the decision permitting publication of
the Third Form-G.
113. We are therefore of the view that the Intervention Applications
cannot be permitted to enlarge the scope of the present Appeal by
introducing questions which were neither the subject matter of IA No.
6304/2024 nor considered by the Adjudicating Authority while passing the
impugned order. Since the Intervention Applications seek consideration of
matters falling outside the controversy arising from the impugned order,
the same cannot be entertained in the present Appeal. Thus, these
applications are not maintainable.

Regulation 28: Transfer of debt due to creditors.
28. (1) In the event a creditor assigns or transfers the debt due to

operates in accordance with the statutory procedure governing recognition
of such assignments and does not retrospectively erase decisions already
taken on the basis of the composition then existing.

124. Based on the discussion in earlier paras, we are of the view that the
earlier approval of the original Resolution Plan did not create an
indefeasible right in favour of the Appellant once the Plan had been
remitted for reconsideration and the revised proposal subsequently failed
to secure the statutory majority. We have further noted that no absolute
statutory prohibition against issuance of a fresh Form-G in the
circumstances of the present case has been demonstrated. The decision of
the CoC to issue fresh Form-G was taken with 100% voting of CoC and it
was only taken after the revised Resolution Plan and the Section 12A
proposal were considered and rejected in 24th and 25th CoC respectively.
The RP and CoC had proceeded in a very systematic manner. When the
matter of issue of fresh Form-G came before CoC, it had only two options,
viz, either continue with the resolution process by issuing a fresh Form-G
or go for the liquidation of the CD. The CoC took a decision in line with the
objectives of the Code that resolution is always preferable to liquidation.
Such decision squarely falls within the domain of the CoC's commercial
decision-making, and has been taken within the statutory framework of
the Code.
