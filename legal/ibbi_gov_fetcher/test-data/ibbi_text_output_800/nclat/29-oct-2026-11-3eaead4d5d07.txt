Findings
24.
We note that the Corporate Debtor was admitted into CIRP on 27.10.2023
on a petition under Section 7 of the Code filed by Pegasus Assets Reconstruction
Private Limited, which holds 100% voting share in the CoC. The plan dated
23.03.2025 of the Pradeep Agrawal Consortium (with an addendum dated
16.02.2026) was first approved by the CoC on 02.04.2025. On 05.12.2025 the
Adjudicating Authority, in IA 1875 of 2025, sent the plans back so that they could
be shared with the suspended Board. The CoC then voted again and approved the
plan with 100% voting share, the result being declared on 04.01.2026.
25.
The plan value is Rs. 21,01,97,888/-, against a fair value of Rs.
20,91,68,637/- and a liquidation value of Rs. 16,61,60,159/-. For provident fund
dues the plan records an amount claimed of Rs. 36,90,344/-, an amount admitted
of Rs. 32,34,223/-, and a provision of Rs. 32,34,223/-, payable upfront within 60
days of the effective date.
26.
EPFO’s own case, is that the Raipur Regional Office filed a claim of Rs.
13,88,701/- on 30.05.2024 and on 16.07.2024 the Resolution Professional asked
for the claim in Form F. The District Office, Bilaspur, passed an order under
Section 7A of the Employees’ Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act,
1952 (“the EPF Act”) on 19.07.2024, and orders under Sections 7Q and 14B on
02.08.2024. A revised claim of Rs. 15,53,780/- was filed on 09.08.2024.

27.
It has been brought out that in the list of creditors uploaded on 30.09.2024,
the claim of the Kolhapur Regional Office of Rs. 21,36,564/- was admitted. Of
the Raipur claim of Rs. 15,53,780/-, Rs. 10,97,659/- was admitted, Rs. 1,77,440/-
was rejected, and Rs. 2,78,681/- was shown as “under verification”. These three

exactly the rejected and the “under verification” amounts together.

knowledge of the Resolution Professionals orders. The Appellant argued that it

It is also the case of the EPFO that provident fund dues are not assets of

31.
The following points arise:
(i)
Whether the appeal was filed within the time allowed by Section 61(2)
of the Code and, if not, whether the delay can be condoned.
(ii)
Whether, in any case, the unpaid sum of Rs. 4,56,121/- is a provident
fund due that the plan was bound to provide for.
We shall deal, both above points in a conjoint manner in the following discussion,
treating as a single point for determination.
32.
At the outset, we need to understand clearly that Section 61(2) of the Code
requires an appeal to be filed within thirty days. Its proviso lets this Appellate
Tribunal to allow the appeal to be filed after thirty days if sufficient cause is
shown, but such period shall not exceed fifteen days. The outer limit is therefore
forty-five days. The Limitation Act, 1963 applies to proceedings under the Code
through Section 238A of the code

[PAGE 15]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1325 of 2026
Page 15 of 20

33.
We further take into consideration that the Honourable Supreme Court
settled the starting point in V. Nagarajan v. SKS Ispat and Power Ltd., (2022) 2
SCC 244. It noted that Section 61(2), unlike Section 421(3) of the Companies
Act, 2013, does not say that time runs from the day a copy of the order is made
available. It held that limitation runs from the date of pronouncement, not from
the date of upload, and that time spent in getting a certified copy is excluded under
Section 12(2) of the Limitation Act only where the party actually applied for the
copy.
34.
On the outer limit, the Honourable Supreme Court in Sagufa Ahmed v.
Upper Assam Plywood Products Pvt. Ltd. (Civil Appeal Nos. 3007-3008 of 2020)
held that the power to condone cannot be stretched beyond the period fixed in the
statute. This Appellate Tribunal has applied the same view in case of Keshav
Kumar Nachani v. Encore Asset Reconstruction Company Pvt. Ltd., Comp.
App. (AT)(Ins) No. 1324 of 2026, the order under appeal was pronounced on
17.04.2026, the thirty days ended on 17.05.2026, the appeal was filed on
29.06.2026, and the application to condone a delay of was dismissed as beyond
the forty-five-day limit.
35.
We now apply this to the dates in the present case. We note that the
Impugned order was pronounced on 28.04.2026. Counting from the next day, the
thirty days ended on 28.05.2026, and the forty-five days ended on 12.06.2026.
EPFO has pleaded for the delay of 26 days. That puts the filing on or about
23.06.2026, which is 56 days after the order and 11 days beyond the outer limit.

[PAGE 16]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1325 of 2026
Page 16 of 20

This is on the delay as stated by EPFO itself. EPFO’s own chronology points the
same way. Even on its account, the appeal was only submitted for approval on
19.06.2026, a week after the outer limit had already run out.
36.
EPFO’s answer is that it did not know of the order until 10.06.2026. That
does not help it. Under Nagarajan (supra), limitation under Section 61(2) does
not depend on when the aggrieved person learns of the order. There is also no
averment that EPFO applied for a certified copy, so Section 12(2) of the
Limitation Act is not available. Section 14 of that Act has no place because no
earlier proceeding was pursued in a wrong forum. The Appellant has also nor
brought as to how any step taken within the forty-five days to obtain the order.
37.
EPFO relies on Regulation 39(5A) of the CIRP Regulations. As inserted
by the Fifth Amendment of 2020, the sub-regulation requires the Resolution
Professional, within fifteen days of the order approving a plan, to intimate each
claimant the principle or formula used for payment of debts under the plan. The
provision does two things we must notice. First, it imposes a duty on the
Resolution Professional after approval. It does not say that a claimant’s time to
appeal begins only when the intimation is received. Second, a regulation made by
the IBBI cannot enlarge a period fixed by an Act of Parliament. A failure to
intimate may expose the Resolution Professional to action before the IBBI but it
cannot extend the statutory period for appeal.
38.
We note that the EPFO had lodged its claim on 30.05.2024 and revised it
on 09.08.2024. The memorandum of appeal narrates in detail how the list of

[PAGE 17]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1325 of 2026
Page 17 of 20

creditors uploaded on 30.09.2024 treated that claim. It does not say when EPFO
first saw that list. If EPFO saw it in or soon after October 2024, it had about
nineteen months to move the Adjudicating Authority under Section 60(5) of the
Code while the CIRP was pending, and gave no explanation for not doing so. If
it never saw the list, it left its own claim unattended for the entire process. A
claimant that has put its claim before a Resolution Professional is expected to
follow what happens to it. Absence of knowledge, arising from such lack of
attention, is not sufficient cause even for the first fifteen days beyond the thirty-
day period. EPFO is a statutory body, but the Code makes no distinction in the
limitation applicable to it.
39.
We therefore find that the appeal was filed after the forty-five day period
in Section 61(2) of the Code had ended, that this Appellate Tribunal has no power
to condone the delay, and that I.A. No. 5178 of 2026 cannot succeed. The appeal
is barred by limitation.
40.
The finding on limitation is enough to dispose of the appeal. But the merits
were argued, the dues arise from the savings of employees, and the question
recurs before this Appellate Tribunal. We therefore decide the second point as
well, so that the result does not rest on a single ground. Our finding on merits is
independent of the finding on limitations.
41.
We find that the law on the protected character of provident fund dues is
settled. Section 36(4)(a)(iii) of the Code excludes from the liquidation estate all
sums due to any workman or employee from the provident fund, pension fund

[PAGE 18]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1325 of 2026
Page 18 of 20

and gratuity fund, and the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Sunil Kumar Jain v.
Sundaresh Bhatt (supra) has held these dues are outside Section 53(1) of the
Code. This Appellate tribunal applied that principle to a resolution plan in Jet
Aircraft Maintenance Engineers Welfare Association (supra) and directed the
successful resolution applicant to pay provident fund dues in full to save the plan
from Section 30(2)(e). The Hon’ble Supreme Court dismissed the challenge to
that order on 30.01.2023 and, in State Bank of India v. Murari Lal Jalan &
Florian Fritsch (Consortium), (2025) 4 SCC 354, again required provident fund
dues to be paid upfront. Section 11(2) of the EPF Act, as read in Maharashtra
State Cooperative Bank Ltd., (2009) 10 SCC 123, covers interest under Section
7Q and damages under Section 14B within “any amount due from an employer”.
42.
Two recent orders of the Hon’ble Supreme Court need to be read together
with these. In Employees Provident Fund Organisation v. Rachna
Jhunjhunwala, Civil Appeal No. 9768 of 2026 (2026 LiveLaw (SC) 749), the
Hon’ble Court upheld a plan that paid the crystallised provident fund contribution
but left out interest under Section 7Q and damages under Section 14B which had
not been determined before the CIRP began. The Hon’ble Supreme Court treated
such claims as contingent liabilities, held that a CoC may or may not provide for
them in its commercial wisdom, and relied on the Committee of Creditors of
Essar Steel India Ltd. v. Satish Kumar Gupta, (2020) 8 SCC 531, for the rule
that a resolution applicant must know the liabilities it takes over.

[PAGE 19]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1325 of 2026
Page 19 of 20

43.
Applying these decisions, we need to first decide. as what the Rs.
4,56,121/- consists of. The record does not say. EPFO’s pleadings give the totals
of the claim, the admitted part, the rejected part and the part under verification,
but not how much of each is contribution under Section 7A, interest under Section
7Q and damages under Section 14B. This matters. The CIRP began on
27.10.2023. The orders under Sections 7A, 7Q and 14B are dated 19.07.2024 and
02.08.2024, that is, nine to ten months after commencement. The first claim, of
Rs. 13,88,701/-, was filed on 30.05.2024, before even the Section 7A order. If any
part of the Rs. 4,56,121/- is interest or damages that were not determined before
27.10.2023, Rachna Jhunjhunwala (supra) would allow the plan to leave it out.
If any part is a contribution that was due and unpaid, the protection under Section
36(4)(a)(iii) of the Code and the decisions on full payment would apply.
44.
The burden of showing that the unpaid part is protected principal lay on
EPFO. It has not shown it. Relief (b) in the appeal is also wider than the grievance:
it asks the respondents to admit and pay the whole Rs. 15,53,780/-, though Rs.
10,97,659/- of it was already admitted and provided for. As against the figures
recorded by the Adjudicating Authority, the plan did provide for all admitted
provident fund dues in full and upfront, which is what Jet Aircraft (supra)
requires. The grievance is about how much was admitted, not about the plan’s
treatment of what was admitted. That grievance was open to challenge earlier.
Under Essar Steel (supra), a claimant dissatisfied with the Resolution
Professional’s decision on its claim may approach the Adjudicating Authority

Once a plan is approved, claims not provided for in it stand extinguished in terms
of ratio Hon’ble Supreme Court as laid in the case of Ghanshyam Mishra and

Company Appeal (AT)(Insolvency) No. 1325 of 2026 is dismissed as
