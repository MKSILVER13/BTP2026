J U D G M E N T
Per Justice Sharad Kumar Sharma Member (Judicial)
1. Before us is the impugned order dated 11.09.2025, which is under an
appellate scrutiny, that was rendered by the Ld. NCLT, Indore, in CP IB
No. 86/2023, which had an effect of admission of the Section 9
application preferred by the Respondent.

[PAGE 2]
Page 2 of 13

2. Primarily, the Ld. Counsel for the Appellant had argued this appeal
from the perspective that, the admission of the Section 9 application,
was based on the viewpoint that, out of the 8 invoices which were made
as subject matter of consideration, as a matter of fact no supply of goods
was ever made under these invoices, and secondly, that since the e-way
bills were not carrying the correct address, it cannot be presumed that,
the goods were actually supplied, which occasion the debt and default
for attracting Section 9 of the Code.

3. The facts that could be borne out from the record are that the Appellant
had alleged that Respondent No. 1 had been in continuous business
transaction of supplying mustard seeds to the Corporate Debtor and in
relation to those transactions had raised 8 invoices, between the period
from 05.08.2021 to 31.08.2021, and in support thereof, the Appellant
had filed the invoices themselves on record. On perusal of the invoices,
which had been placed before the Ld. Tribunal, what could be seen is
that the invoices do carry the address registered as the address of the
Corporate Debtor, but the only distinction or the difference, which has
been admitted by the Appellant is that the invoices did not carry the
correct PIN Code. It is because of that reason that it is argued by the
Ld. Counsel for the Appellant before the Ld. Tribunal that there was a
wrong address mentioned in the invoices and, hence, the goods were
not supplied. Thus there cannot be a presumptive inference of debt and
default, when on its basis the 8 invoices were argued to be doubtful.

[PAGE 3]
Page 3 of 13

4. The transactions under the aforesaid 8 invoices were carried between
the period from 05.08.2021 to 31.08.2021, wherein the default amount
was shown to be of Rs. 01,42,46,178/-, was alleged to be payable, along
with interest payable on it @ 12% P.A., that is, Rs. 28,58,480/-.
According to Part IV of the Section 8 notice that was issued, the date of
default was shown to be 20.08.2021, and was determined to be from
the date of the first invoice. In accordance with the notice under Section
8, which was issued on 10.05.2023, as per the contents of Part I, the
amount shown to be due was above the threshold limit as contemplated
under Section 4 of the IBC Code, and in accordance with the date of
default that has been expressed therein, the default was disclosed to be
as on 20.08.2021. Admittedly, and as per records too the Section 9
application was preferred on 20.07.2023, as against the Corporate
Debtor, which was registered as CP (IB No. 86/2023), before the Ld.
NCLT. Its during the course of the proceedings, that the Corporate
Debtor came out with the case that there was no actual or complete
supply made of the goods undertaken to be supplied under the
respective invoices. As there was no agreement in writing, nor any
agreement was entitling for payment of interest. Another discrepancy,
which was pointed out by the Corporate Debtor in his reply was with
regard to the e-way bills carrying a wrong address, and hence based on
it, a presumption was drawn that there was no supply of the goods at
all and, thus, there was no default.

[PAGE 4]
Page 4 of 13

5. Further, it was argued that in the absence of there being any agreement
or any purchase order or any acceptance or acknowledgment, as to
whether actually the goods were actually supplied to the Respondent,
there could not be said to be any justification to conclude of existence
of the debt and default which could have necessitated the initiation of
proceedings under Section 9 of the Code. Besides that, the GST returns
for August 2021, nowhere it clarifies or has a segregated supply. It is
based upon the aforesaid premise that the proceedings were taken, and
in the proceedings that were held on 25.04.2025, before Ld NCLT, it
had directed the Corporate Debtor to answer the queries pertaining to
the TDS deduction and Income Tax Credit on GST and sought an
explanation from the Appellant.
6. What is more important is that the Appellant did file an affidavit in
which he explained that the Corporate Debtor had deducted the TDS
on purchase, and the Corporate Debtor had also claimed the benefit of
input tax credit for GST paid by the Operational Creditor. More
importantly, besides that, the ledger account itself mentions an amount
due to be paid, that is, Rs. 01,29,33,407/-, as on the date of the
application, which was above the threshold limit.
7. On an overall summary of the documents, these were produced and
upon its scrutiny, what could be inferred is that there had been supply
made under the invoices, were infact was received by the Appellant,

[PAGE 5]
Page 5 of 13

because of the fact that the TDS deductions were shown to have been
made against the invoices was evident from the records. Non-supply of
goods as against the aforesaid 8 invoices cannot be a theory which
would acceptable to us, for the reason being that under normal
business prudence, wherein the Appellant it had raised invoices and
the goods were not supplied against it, in that eventuality, it was all the
more necessary under simple logic the appellant ought to establish that
the Appellant did raise a query because of the non-supply of goods, and
if it is not done, the presumption would be that the goods under the 8
invoices were supplied, owing to the ledger entries, the TDS deductions,
and with regard to the GST returns for August 2021 as against the
goods supplied.
8. What is more important is that the Appellant, very fairly, during the
course of the argument, accepted that:-
1)
Apart from the registered office, the address of which was disclosed
in the notice and in the invoices too, he had no other registered or
unregistered office or godown from where he operated his business.
2)
He submitted, that the only difference in the address given in the
documents, was pertaining to the PIN Code.
3)
We are of the view that the difference in the PIN Code in itself cannot
lead to any positive conclusive inference, that the goods were not
supplied. He further admits the fact, as per the invoices, he had
never raised any query with the Respondent about non-supply of
goods or non-receipt of goods, and if that was the situation, the

[PAGE 6]

natural consequence would be that the goods were supplied. Even
as per the documents too which was filed by the Appellant, if we see
the e-way bill, that has been placed on record, the addresses given
therein correspond to the address of the Appellant, except for the
PIN Code No. The transactions that took place as against the 8
invoices as well as one of the invoices showing address are detailed

This itself shows that once there was an input tax credit claimed on the
part of the GST paid as invoiced by the supplier, all these documents did show
that the goods were actually supplied and received by the Appellant, and the
minor change in the PIN Code in the invoices for the demand notice will not
have any adverse bearing on the proceedings under Section 9 of the IBC Code.
Because of the fact that the supply of the goods is accepted by the Appellant,
its receipt, once these aspects are established, and because of the fact that
the Appellant's defence had been exclusively that there was no supply, ruled
out from the documents on record, and it has been ruled out too with regard
to as to what effect the address would have because of non-reference of the
PIN Code.
11. On the issue of threshold, when the Tribunal passed an order on
25.04.2025 directing the Appellant to explain the information pertaining to
the ledger copy of the supplier of the Operational Creditor bearing in the books
of accounts for the aforesaid period of transaction, the Tribunal, based upon
the affidavit filed by the Corporate Debtor on 20.06.2025, rightly came to a
conclusion that, according to the ledger entries supplied by the Appellant
himself, the ledger account, from the books of the Corporate Debtor showed
an outstanding amount as on the date of filing of the application was Rs.
1,29,33,407/- as against an amount of Rs. 1,42,46,179/- as stated in the
application filed by the Operational Creditor. It is because of that the
conclusion, which could be drawn is that, in the circumstances where:-

[PAGE 13]
Page 13 of 13

1) CD admits the supply when the supply stands established by the
deduction of the TDS on purchase as per Section 194(q) of the Income
Tax Act.
2) When the Corporate Debtor had admitted the claim of input tax credit
of GST.
3) When the ledger account shows the balance of Rs. 1,29,33,407/-, it
would obviously be falling above the threshold limit as contemplated
under Section 4 of the Code.
4) The request for reconciliation was rightly not considered by the Ld.
Tribunal because, once the document itself had established the debt
and default and the grounds remained substantially rebutted, the
admission of the Section 9 application as against the Appellant was in
accordance with the provisions of law.
12. For the aforesaid reasons, the impugned order does not call for any
interference by us in the exercise of our appellate jurisdiction. The appeal
would stand rejected, and all pending Interlocutory Applications would stand
closed.
