NARESH SALECHA, MEMBER (TECHNICAL)
1.
The present appeal has been filed by the Appellant i.e., Mr. Rajesh
Harikishan Khandelwal who is Suspended Management of MIM Petroworld Pvt.
Ltd. under Section 61 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (‘Code’)
against the Order dated 27.08.2026 ("Impugned Order") passed by the Hon’ble
National Company Law Tribunal, Ahmedabad in CP (IB) No.: 252 of 2023.
Jay Ambe Industries Through its Authorized Person Rajeshkumar
Harkishandas Khemani is the Respondent No.1 herein.
Mr. Bhavik Haribhai Rupapara who is the Interim Resolution Professional
of Appellant, is the Respondent No.2 herein.
2.
The Appellant submitted that the Respondent had filed the aforesaid
Company Petition under Section 9 of the Code claiming an alleged operational
debt of Rs. 1,96,00,535/-, comprising principal amount of Rs. 1,77,32,898/- and

[PAGE 3]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1700 of 2026

Page 3 of 37

interest of Rs. 18,67,637/- calculated at 18% per annum, alleging defaults
between 12.01.2023 and 17.01.2023. Prior thereto, the Respondent had issued a
demand notice dated 17.08.2023 under Section 8 of the Code, which was duly
replied to by the Appellant on 29.08.2023. In the said reply, the Appellant had
specifically brought to the Respondent’s notice the existence of a pre-existing
dispute concerning the inferior and sub-standard quality of goods supplied by the
Respondent.
3.
The Appellant contended that it was engaged in the business of trading
Mixed Mineral Hydrocarbon Oil and procuring the same from suppliers for
onward supply to its end-customers, who utilised such goods in specialised and
heavy vehicles employed in capital-intensive projects. The Appellant had an
established business reputation and was regularly servicing its financial
obligations. At the relevant time, the Appellant had a turnover of approximately
Rs. 12,260.53 lakhs and had maintained financial discipline in its dealings with
its suppliers, financial creditors and customers. The Appellant further submitted
that it had no outstanding operational creditor other than the disputed claim of the
Respondent and that its banking facilities were being regularly serviced.
4.
The Appellant submitted that the business relationship between the parties
was conducted substantially through Mr. Uday Jatin Shah, who represented
himself as a partner/person concerned with the day-to-day affairs and decision-
making of the Respondent. The material correspondence and business dealings
between the parties were conducted through him. Acting upon the Respondent’s

[PAGE 4]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1700 of 2026

Page 4 of 37

representations regarding the quality and supply of Mixed Mineral Hydrocarbon
Oil, the Appellant commenced placing orders with the Respondent from January
2022. During Financial Year 2021-22, the Appellant procured goods worth
approximately Rs. 16.14 crores and, up to December 2022, goods worth
approximately Rs. 54.41 crores in Financial Year 2022-23.
5.
The Appellant contended that during November and December 2022, the
goods supplied by the Respondent were found to be extremely sub-standard and
unfit for the purpose for which they had been procured. The use of such material
resulted in heavy vehicles and equipment of the Appellant’s customers coming to
a standstill, which in turn disrupted the functioning of capital-intensive plants and
industries. The Appellant immediately communicated the quality issues to the
Respondent, whereupon the Respondent assured the Appellant that the
consequences would be resolved and that quality goods would be supplied in
future. Relying upon such assurances, the Appellant continued its business
dealings with the Respondent.
6.
The Appellant further submitted that notwithstanding the assurances given
by the Respondent, further consignments were supplied and again complaints
regarding sub-standard material were received from the Appellant’s end-
customers. The machinery and vehicles using the goods started failing and,
ultimately, on 18.12.2022, the machines completely stopped functioning owing
to the poor quality of the product. Consequently, the invoice dated 17.12.2022
was returned on 24.12.2022. The Appellant contended that the Respondent had

[PAGE 5]

assured both supply of good quality material and resolution of the earlier quality-
related complaints, but neither assurance was honoured.
7.
The Appellant submitted that the quality complaint was not merely an oral
assertion raised subsequently as a defence to the insolvency proceedings. The
Appellant had referred the sub-standard batches to laboratory testing agencies,
whose reports confirmed that the goods were of inferior quality and had failed the
required threshold of usability. The laboratory reports were communicated to the
Respondent and were never specifically controverted as to their existence, receipt
or substantive contents. The Appellant further submitted that the laboratory
material was accompanied
by photographs, videography and other
contemporaneous material evidencing the condition of the goods.
8.
The Appellant contended that WhatsApp was the established and accepted
mode of business communication between the parties. Orders were placed,
deliveries were coordinated, invoices and payment details were communicated
and, most importantly, complaints regarding the quality of the goods were raised
through WhatsApp. Thus, the WhatsApp communications formed part of the
ordinary course of business between the parties and could not have been
discarded merely because the communications were not exchanged through
formal corporate emails. The Appellant submitted that the quality dispute was
communicated contemporaneously and substantially before issuance of the
statutory demand notice.

9.
The Appellant further contended that the Respondent itself acknowledged
the quality-related difficulties and sought return/retrieval of the goods. The
communications also demonstrated that the Respondent was aware of the
complaints received by the Appellant from its customers. The Appellant
submitted that despite the Respondent’s failure to resolve the issue, the Appellant
had continued making payments and had, in the course of business, already paid
approximately Rs. 5.5 crores to the Respondent. After the quality dispute was
communicated, the Appellant also paid approximately Rs. 76 lakhs, returned
goods worth approximately Rs. 30–40 lakhs and incurred approximately Rs. 3–4
lakhs towards logistics costs, thereby demonstrating that the dispute was confined
to the defective goods and was not a sham defence to avoid an admitted liability.
10.
The Appellant submitted that the consequences of the defective supplies
were substantial. Heavy-duty vehicles and machinery of its customers suffered
breakdowns and the functioning of their plants was affected. The Appellant
consequently suffered severe business and reputational losses, while its major
customers claimed reimbursement, compensation and other adjustments. The
Appellant contended that the losses attributable to the sub-standard goods were
substantially higher than the amount claimed by the Respondent in the Section 9
proceedings.
11.
The Appellant contended that the Respondent did not specifically
controvert the existence of the quality dispute or the documents relied upon by
the Appellant in its rejoinder. The Adjudicating Authority, by its order dated

communication dated 14.08.2023 referred to by the Adjudicating Authority was
not available on record and had not been filed by either party, and that the matter
was remanded for the limited purpose of placing the relevant documents on
record.
13.
The Appellant submitted that pursuant to the remand, it was granted liberty
on 27.04.2026 to place the relevant documents and communications on record
and accordingly filed an Additional Affidavit dated 13.06.2026 containing

14.
The Appellant contended that despite the aforesaid material having been

by non-application of mind and failure to appreciate the material facts and
documents placed before the Adjudicating Authority.
15.
The Appellant submitted that the Adjudicating Authority fundamentally
erred in applying a standard of proof akin to final adjudication of the underlying
commercial dispute. It was contended that, while considering a Section 9
application, the Adjudicating Authority was required to examine whether a
plausible dispute existed which required further investigation and whether the
dispute was genuine and not spurious, hypothetical or illusory. The Adjudicating
Authority could not have undertaken a detailed trial into the ultimate merits,
evidentiary value or conclusiveness of the Appellant’s defence regarding the
quality of the goods.

16.
The Appellant further contended that the Adjudicating Authority erred in
holding that the quality dispute could not constitute a pre-existing dispute because
no specific stipulation concerning quality was contained in the invoices or
purchase documents. It was submitted that no formal purchase contract
containing such stipulation had been produced by either party and the parties had
admittedly conducted their business through an established course of dealing. The
absence of a formal contractual clause concerning quality could not, therefore,
extinguish a dispute arising from the actual supply of defective goods.
17.
The Appellant submitted that the Adjudicating Authority further failed to
appreciate the admitted Bill-to-Ship arrangement between the parties. Under the
established course of business, goods ordered by the Appellant from the
Respondent were directly delivered to the Appellant’s end-customers. The tax
invoices, e-way bills and delivery documents reflected destinations different from
the Appellant’s registered/business address. Therefore, the fact that the goods
were received by end-customers could not convert the quality dispute into a
dispute between unrelated third parties. The complaints, debit notes and losses
suffered by the end-customers were directly attributable to the goods supplied by
the Respondent under the very transactions forming the subject matter of the
Section 9 petition.
18.
The Appellant contended that the Adjudicating Authority also erred in

The Appellant submitted that the dispute regarding defective goods had been

FINDINGS
71.
This appeal is filed by the suspended Director of MIM Petro World Private
Limited / the Corporate Debtor. It challenges the order dated 27.08.2026 of the
Adjudicating Authority in CP (IB) No. 252 of 2023. By that order, the
Adjudicating Authority admitted the Section 9 petition of Jay Ambe
Industries/the Operational Creditor/Respondent No. 1 and began the CIRP.
72.
We have been informed that the Operational Creditor sold Mixed Mineral
Hydrocarbon Oil to the Corporate Debtor. We note that the claim is for invoices
dated 13.12.2022 to 18.12.2022, totalling Rs. 1,77,32,898, with interest of Rs.
18,67,637 at 18% up to 17.08.2023, making Rs. 1,96,00,535. Payment was due
after 30 days, and default is stated as 12.01.2023 to 17.01.2023. Nothing earlier
than December 2022 is claimed.
73.
We further note that the Adjudicating Authority first rejected the petition
on 08.11.2024, holding that a quality dispute existed before the notice. By order
dated 06.04.2026, this Appellate Tribunal set aside the order in the Operational
Creditor’s appeal and remitted the matter to the Adjudicating Authority for fresh
decision by a reasoned order after hearing both sides. The reason was that the lab

[PAGE 29]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1700 of 2026

Page 29 of 37

test report and a communication said to be dated 14.08.2023 were not available
on record, and the dispute could not be finally decided without them. After the
remand, the Corporate Debtor filed an additional affidavit dated 13.06.2026 with
WhatsApp chats and the lab report, and the Operational Creditor filed a rejoinder.
The Adjudicating Authority then admitted the petition.
74.
At this stage it needs to be appreciated that an operational creditor can start
CIRP only for an operational debt that is due and not disputed. The debtor may,
within ten days of the demand notice, bring to the creditor’s notice the existence
of a dispute, if any in terms of the Section 8(2)(a)) of the code. If the creditor has
received a notice of dispute, the application may be rejected in terms of the
Section 9(5)(ii)(d)) of the code. Section 5(6) of the code also stipulates that a
dispute includes a suit or arbitration relating to the existence of the debt, the
quality of goods or service, or breach of a representation or warranty. In Mobilox
Innovations Pvt. Ltd. v. Kirusa Software Pvt. Ltd., (2018) 1 SCC 353, the
Hon’ble Supreme Court held that the definition is inclusive, so a dispute need not
have reached a court or arbitrator. It is enough that a real dispute existed before
the notice was received. The test, laid down in Mobilox (supra), is whether there
is a plausible contention that requires further investigation, and whether the
dispute is genuine and not spurious, hypothetical or illusory. It is settled law by
now that the adjudicating authority does not weigh the evidence or decide who
will win. It separates the grain from the chaff, and rejects the defence only if it is
a patently feeble legal argument or an assertion of fact unsupported by evidence.

[PAGE 30]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1700 of 2026

Page 30 of 37

75.
We take into consideration that the Hon’ble Supreme Court applied this
test again in GLS Films Industries Pvt. Ltd. v. Chemical Suppliers India Pvt.
Ltd., 2026 INSC 344. The debtor therefore alleged defective supplies and the
need to reconcile accounts. The Adjudicating Authority rejected the Section 9
petition, NCLAT reversed after examining the merits of the defence, and the
Supreme Court restored the Adjudicating Authority’s order. It held that the
authority has only to be satisfied that a plausible pre-existing dispute exists, and
that whether the party raising it will succeed is not within the ken of such inquiry.
76.
We endorse the propositions that Section 9 petition is not a suit for
recovery, and a genuinely contested debt cannot be collected through insolvency.
Equally, a bare denial does not defeat the petition. The Adjudicating Authority
therefore need not to decide whether the goods were defective but to decide
whether the record shows a plausible dispute that existed before the notice.
77.
This Appellate Tribunal vide order dated 06.04.2026 directed fresh
adjudication by a reasoned order by the Adjudicating Authority and we did not
decide the dispute for either side. The Adjudicating Authority was free to look at
the matter afresh with the new documents, and therefore the Adjudicating
Authority was not barred from re-appreciating the material. This Appellate
Tribunal then noted that the Operational Creditor had not specifically denied the
WhatsApp screenshots and had only called them selective; that the lab test report
was not specifically denied, though its timing was disputed; and that the debit
notes of Hiltop Highrise Pvt. Ltd. appeared relevant while that of Reliable

[PAGE 31]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1700 of 2026

Page 31 of 37

Industries appeared unrelated. To take a different view, the Adjudicating
Authority had to say why, by pointing to what the Operational Creditor had said
or shown since. The impugned order refers to none of this and gives no such
reason.
78.
We note that the Operational Creditor itself accepted that the goods under
the invoice dated 17.12.2022 were returned on 24.12.2022, a week after supply
and nearly eight months before the demand notice. The Operational Creditor
submitted that this invoice was not part of its claim. That is an answer on the
amount. It does not remove the fact that goods supplied in mid-December 2022
were sent back by the buyer long before any demand. The invoice also falls in the
very week of the claimed invoices, and the impugned order records no invoice-
wise comparison. The Operational Creditor’s own case, noted in paragraph 13(e)
of the order, is that the return of material is already accounted for. That is itself
an acceptance that returns had to be adjusted before the sum due could be fixed.
79.
We find that the Adjudicating Authority found in paragraph 13(c) that the
WhatsApp chats were dated before the demand notice. They run from November
2022 and carry photographs, videos and the lab report. The Operational Creditor
alleges that the dispute was created by bringing in a third party, but it has not
shown that the messages were never sent or were altered. Its objections are that
they are not invoice-specific, were sent to a third person and lack a certificate
under Section 65B. On this issue, we are of the opinion that a complaint of
selectivity can be answered by producing the rest of the chat, and the record does

[PAGE 32]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1700 of 2026

Page 32 of 37

not show that any other chat was placed to show that the complaints were absent
or answered. We put a direct query to the Respondent to produce full WhatsApp
chat from his record, even at the stage of hearing before us, which he could not
do and reasons given is that due to change in mobile, he does not have chats with
him. Therefore, we are not in position to accept the contentions of the Respondent
on this issue.
80.
It is noted that the reply of 29.08.2023 was sent in time by the corporate
debtor and the Corporate Debtor raised the issue of quality of the goods and is
consistent with the chats and the December 2022 return. Thus, we are of prima
facie view that it is not a story first told after the notice. It is a notice of dispute
under Section 9(5)(ii)(d), resting on events some eight months older. The
Operational Creditor claims nothing for the months before December 2022, and
by its own account the last sale was on 18.12.2022. No supply followed the return
and the complaints. That fits the Corporate Debtor’s case that it stopped buying
and paying after a bad batch, and gives a plausible reason for withholding
payment. The Operational Creditor’s answer, that the goods were checked by the
Corporate Debtor before leaving its premises and that any dispute lies between
the Corporate Debtor and its customers, goes to who is responsible. It does not
say that no complaint was made. Responsibility is a question for trial.
81.
We find that the Adjudicating Authority recorded no finding that any
identifiable part of the claim, above the threshold of Rs. 1 crore, stands apart from
the dispute. The complaint relates to the November–December 2022 supplies,

[PAGE 33]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1700 of 2026

Page 33 of 37

which include the invoices claimed. On these facts, we are of view that the
contention of the Corporate Debtor is not spurious, hypothetical or illusory. It
rests on an admitted return of goods, chats dated before the notice that were not
specifically denied, and a reply sent in time. It needs investigation, and that is
enough to take the case out of Section 9.
82.
We have examined the Impugned order and note that the Adjudicating
Authority took the opposite view in paragraph 13(a) to (g). None of its reasons
can stand for the following reasons.
In para 13 (a), the Adjudicating Authority stated that there was no quality
term in the invoice or contract. However, we find that the Code does not require
a dispute to be foreseen in a contract, and Section 5(6)(b) names the quality of
goods itself. No purchase contract was produced by the Operational Creditor
either, though it sues on the invoices, so the Corporate Debtor cannot be faulted
for not producing what neither side produced. The law also fills the gaps as under
Section 16(2) of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930, goods bought by description from
a seller who deals in them must be of merchantable quality. A buyer needs no
written clause to complain of unusable goods.
In para 13 (b) the Adjudicating Authority mentioned Third-party disputes.
The order treats the complaints of the Corporate Debtor’s customers, and its
resulting losses, as matters with third parties, and notes that the e-way bills and
invoices name only the Corporate Debtor as recipient. This overlooks the
admitted bill-to-ship basis, under which the invoice is raised on the buyer and the

[PAGE 34]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1700 of 2026

Page 34 of 37

goods go straight to its customer. Naming the Corporate Debtor is exactly what
one would expect, and says nothing against the dispute. Goods that never reach
the buyer’s godown can only be complained of by the people who use them, and
the complaint then travels back up the chain. The complaint is still about the
Operational Creditor’s goods. Whether the Corporate Debtor can recover its
losses is separate, and we do not decide it.
Similarly in para 13 (c), the Adjudicating authority stated that only one
invoice was returned. We are of view that the number of returns bears on the size
of the dispute, not on its existence. Under the bill-to-ship model, the goods were
with the Corporate Debtor’s customers, and it could not be expected to send them
back itself.
On examination of para 13 (d), where the Adjudicating authority discussed
the communication of 14.08.2023, the Impugned order draws an adverse
inference because this document was not produced even after the remand. The
Appellant explains that the first order of the Adjudicating authority dated
08.11.2024 wrongly refers to the demand notice as dated 14.08.2023, when it is
dated 17.08.2023. We find this explanation is plausible and fits the record, and
neither side has produced any communication of that date. A document dated
three days before the notice could not outweigh the material of November and
December 2022 in any case. An adverse inference for not producing a document
of doubtful existence cannot take the place of the Mobilox test.

[PAGE 35]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins.) No. 1700 of 2026

Page 35 of 37

The Adjudicating authority in para 13 (e) discussed the lab test report and
held that the report did not show the institution, date or identity. We are of the
view that these go to the weight of the report at trial and do not decide whether a
plausible dispute exists when the report is one part of a larger picture. The
Operational Creditor’s challenge, as recorded, is to the form, source and timing
of the report, and not that the goods passed any test.
In para 13 (f), the Adjudicating Authority discards the chats because they
were addressed to Mr. Uday, who is said not to be an authorized person of the
Operational Creditor. The Operational Creditor denies that he is its partner, while
the Corporate Debtor says that he dealt with orders, invoices and deliveries. This
Appellate Tribunal noted on 06.04.2026 that the complaints were made first to
Mr. Uday and then to the Operational Creditor, and the Appellant points to
messages addressed to the proprietor himself, which the impugned order does not
address. His authority is a disputed fact that cannot be settled in summary
proceedings. The Operational Creditor’s own acceptance of the December 2022
return suggests that the complaint reached it in some form.
Finally in para 13 (g), the Adjudicating authority noted that the Hiltop
Highrise debit note was dated 22.08.2023, after the demand notice, and covers
purchases of April–May 2023, long after the last sale on 18.12.2022. The Reliable
Industries debit note was noted as unrelated. We place no reliance on either, and
our conclusion stands without them.

83.
Taken together, these reasons show that the Adjudicating Authority in
effect tried the defence. It asked whether the Corporate Debtor had proved that the
goods were bad, that Mr. Uday had authority, that the lab report was genuine and
that its customers’ claims were valid, and it held that the Corporate Debtor failed
to establish a genuine dispute. We are of the considered view that these are
questions for a trial. The Adjudicating Authority did not ask whether there was a
plausible contention needing investigation. The Corporate Debtor did not have to
prove its case. It had to show that its case was plausible and not an afterthought,
and on this record it did.
84.
We hold that a plausible dispute about the quality of the goods existed
before the demand notice was received. It rests on the admitted return of goods on
24.12.2022, the chats of November–December 2022 that were not specifically
denied, and the reply of 29.08.2023. The Adjudicating Authority applied a
contractual requirement that the Code does not impose, treated a bill-to-ship
dispute as a third-party matter, tested the lab report and Mr. Uday’s authority as at
trial, and drew an adverse inference from a document of doubtful existence. The
impugned order dated 27.08.2026 therefore cannot be sustained.
85.
We find merit in the appeal. The appeal succeeds. The Impugned order is
set aside. No order to cost. IA, if any, stands closed. We however would place on
record that above findings are confined to the question of pre-existing dispute at
the Section 9 stage and we have not expressed any opinion on whether the goods
were defective or on the Corporate Debtor’s claim of loss, or on the amount due to
