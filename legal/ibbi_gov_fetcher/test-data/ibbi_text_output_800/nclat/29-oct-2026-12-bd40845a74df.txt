Findings
35.
This appeal is filed by the Deputy Commissioner of Commercial Taxes
(Audit)-1, Mangaluru, against the order under which the Adjudicating Authority
dismissed the Appellant’s application under Section 60(5) read with Section 42 of
the Code. In that application the Appellant asked for three reliefs. First, that the
Liquidator’s communication dated 02.05.2026, which rejected its Proof of Claim

[PAGE 16]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1752 of 2026

Page 16 of 25

in Form C dated 22.04.2026, be set aside. Second, that a delay of 896 days in filing
the claim be condoned. Third, that the Liquidator be directed to verify and admit
the claim arising from the order dated 21.02.2026 passed under Section 74(9) of
the CGST/KGST Acts.
36.
At the outset, it may be clarified that on 08.10.2026 learned counsel for the
Respondents asked that the appeal be decided at the admission stage without a
reply, as the issue is short. The Appellant also agreed, as such we heard both sides
and reserved judgment at the admission stage itself.
37.
We have not set out the pleadings and arguments of the Respondents
separately. Every point raised is being dealt with in the findings below, and the
appeal is being decided finally.
38.
We note that the Corporate Debtor was admitted into CIRP on 31.08.2021.
It was ordered to be liquidated on 03.10.2023 in IA/769(AHM)/2021, and
Sumedha Management Solutions Private Limited was appointed as Liquidator. A
public announcement in Form B was made on 11.10.2023, and the last date for
submitting claims was fixed as 08.11.2023. It is undisputed fact that the Appellant
did not file any claim by that date.
39.
The dues relate to the period April 2019 to March 2020. In that period the
Corporate Debtor was registered under the CGST and KGST Acts and made
taxable supplies. On scrutiny of its returns, the Appellant noticed that IGST had
been paid on intra-State supplies. Adjudication proceedings under Section 74 of
the CGST/KGST Acts were after the liquidation order. The Appellant issued the

[PAGE 17]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1752 of 2026

Page 17 of 25

Show Cause Notice to the Corporate Debtor on 23.09.2025. The Liquidator
replied to the notice on 23.10.2025 and 14.11.2025 and informed the Appellant
about the liquidation. It has been brought out that on 21.02.2026, an order was
passed by the Appellant, under Section 74(9), read with Sections 50 and 122 of
the CGST/KGST Acts, fixing the liability of the corporate debtor at Rs.8,10,000/-
, made up of CGST of Rs.4,05,000/- and KGST of Rs.4,05,000/-. A rectification
application filed by the Corporate Debtor on 26.02.2026 was rejected on
24.03.2026.
40.
We have been informed that the Appellant filed its Proof of Claim in Form
C on 22.04.2026. The Liquidator rejected it on 02.05.2026 because it was filed
after the prescribed period. The application before the Adjudicating Authority
was by the Appellant on 15.05.2026, which is within fourteen days of the
rejection. It is significant to note that the claim was filed 896 days after the last
date of 08.11.2023.
41.
The Appellant had earlier filed IA No. 680(AHM)/2025 relating to
proceedings under Section 73 of the Acts. It was dismissed on 21.11.2025.
Company Appeal (AT) (Ins.) No. 681 of 2026 against that dismissal is pending.
That appeal is not before us and nothing in this judgment touches it.
42.
Based on the above facts, four questions arise. One, whether a claim based
on the order dated 21.02.2026 can be admitted in a liquidation that began on
03.10.2023. Two, whether the delay of 896 days deserves to be condoned. Three,
whether the judgment of the Supreme Court in Sundaresh Bhatt, Liquidator of

[PAGE 18]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1752 of 2026

Page 18 of 25

ABG Shipyard v. Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs, Civil Appeal
No. 7667 of 2021, (“ABG Shipyard”), relied on by the Appellant, helps the
Appellant. Four, whether the other objections taken by the parties call for a
decision.
43.
We note that Section 38(1) of the Code requires the liquidator to receive
claims within thirty days from the commencement of the liquidation process.
Regulation 12 of the IBBI (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 requires a
public announcement calling upon all stakeholders to submit their claims as on
the liquidation commencement date. It fixes the last date at thirty days from that
date. Regulation 16 requires a person claiming to be a stakeholder to submit the
claim as on the liquidation commencement date, on or before the last date. The
liquidator then verifies the claims under Regulation 30 within thirty days of the
last date.
44.
We further take into consideration that under Sections 39 and 40 of the
Code, the liquidator verifies the claims and admits or rejects them. Under Section
42 of the Code, a creditor aggrieved by the decision of the liquidator may appeal
to the Adjudicating Authority within fourteen days of receiving the decision. So,
both ends of the process carry short and fixed time limits. The Code does this on
purpose. Liquidation is meant to move in a fixed order and within a fixed time,
so that the list of stakeholders is settled early and distribution can follow.
45.
We consciously note that the Regulations do not give the liquidator any
power to accept a claim after the last date. The Appellant also accepts this. The

[PAGE 19]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1752 of 2026

Page 19 of 25

Liquidator was therefore right to reject the claim as late. The real question is what
the Adjudicating Authority, and now this Appellate Tribunal, can and should do
about it.
46.
It is taken into consideration that Section 3(6) of the Code defines a “claim”
as a right to payment, whether or not it has been reduced to judgment, fixed,
disputed or undisputed. Section 5(21) of the Code treats dues payable to a State
Government under any law as operational debt. The State tax authority to whom
such dues are owed is an operational creditor. It is a creditor like any other. Its
dues have a place in the waterfall under Section 53 of the Code, but only if they
come in through the process the Code lays down.
47.
At this stage we find that the Appellant’s case needs a closer look at what
Section 42 of the Code gives, which lets a creditor challenge the liquidator’s
decision to admit or reject a claim. Thus, we find that it is a remedy against a
decision, however, it does not create a fresh right to file a claim that the
Regulations have closed. The Adjudicating Authority, while hearing a Section 42
of the Code application, can check whether the liquidator acted within the Code
and the Regulations. Here the Liquidator did exactly that, and applied Regulation
12 and refused a claim that came after the last date.
48.
It is undisputed fact that Section 60(5) of the Code gives the Adjudicating
Authority wide jurisdiction over claims by or against the corporate debtor and
over any question of law or fact arising out of or in relation to the insolvency
resolution or liquidation. That power is real. But it is a power to do justice within

[PAGE 20]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1752 of 2026

Page 20 of 25

the Code. It is not a power to rewrite the time limits that the Code and the
Regulations have fixed. A wide power must still be used in a way that fits the
scheme of the statute.
49.
The Appellant also pointed to Section 238A of the Code, under which the
Limitation Act applies to proceedings before the Adjudicating Authority. That
does not help in this case. The Appellant’s difficulty is not that its Section 42 of
the Code application was filed late. It was filed in time. The difficulty is that the
claim itself was filed 896 days after the last date fixed under the Regulations. The
Limitation Act does not fix a period for filing a claim before a liquidator, and
Section 5 of the Code cannot be used to stretch a period that the Code fixes for a
different step.
50.
The Appellant relied heavily on ABG Shipyard (Supra). In that case the
Honourable Supreme Court considered whether the Customs authorities could
proceed against goods of a corporate debtor in liquidation by issuing demand
notices and moving to sell the goods. The Honourable Supreme Court held that
demand notices issued during the moratorium under Section 14 or Section 33(5)
of the Code violate the Code. It also drew a line between assessment and recovery.
Relying on S.V. Kondaskar v. V.M. Deshpande, AIR 1972 SC 878, the
Honourable Supreme Court held that an authority may continue to determine the
tax, interest, fine or penalty due, but cannot enforce recovery during the
moratorium.

[PAGE 21]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1752 of 2026

Page 21 of 25

51.
Thus, we observe that the ABG Shipyard (supra) decides two things. The
authority may assess during the moratorium, but it may not recover. And once it
has assessed, it must claim within the time the Code allows. The judgment does
not stipulate that the time for filing a claim starts afresh on the date of assessment.
If it did, a tax authority would have no time limit at all, and the direction to follow
the time periods in the Code would mean nothing.
52.
We have noted that the Appellant’s answer to the delay is that its liability
was fixed only on 21.02.2026, and that it filed the claim within a reasonable time
after that. There are two ways of looking at this, and the Appellant fails on both.
53.
The first view is that the claim existed from the tax period itself. The
Corporate Debtor’s liability for April 2019 to March 2020 arose from its supplies
in that period. It had not been quantified, but Section 3(6) of the Code stipulates
that a right to payment is a claim even when it is not fixed or is disputed. On this
view, the claim existed long before the CIRP began on 31.08.2021, and certainly
on 03.10.2023. The Appellant could have filed it by 08.11.2023 with the
particulars it had. It did not. The Appellant says it assigned the Section 74
proceedings of the CGST/KGST Acts only on 12.08.2024. That shows when the
Appellant chose to look at these returns. A tax authority’s own delay in
scrutinising returns cannot be passed on to a liquidation that has been running on
public notice. On this view the claim is simply late by 896 days.
54.
The second view is the Appellant’s own case, that the liability came into
existence only on 21.02.2026. On this view the claim rests on an order passed

[PAGE 22]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1752 of 2026

Page 22 of 25

more than two years after the liquidation commencement date. This Appellate
Tribunal has held more than once that such a claim cannot be admitted. It has
been held that Regulations 12 and 16 contemplate claims as on the liquidation
commencement date, and that a claim arising later cannot be admitted. The same
view was taken for claims founded on assessment orders passed after liquidation
began.
55.
We do not need to choose between the two views, because the result is the
same. On the first view the claim is late and no good reason is shown requiring
any intervention. On the second view the claim lies outside Regulations 12 and
16 and cannot be admitted, whatever the reason for the delay. Whichever way it
is seen, the Appellant cannot succeed.
56.
The Appellant also pleaded and wanted us to accept his logic that
proceedings under Sections 73 and 74 of the CGST/KGST Acts are different, and
relied on PEI Industries v. Union of India, W.P (C) No. 7725 of 2026 decided by
the Delhi High Court on 29.05.2026. Even if we accept the arguments of the
Appellant that they are different under the CGST/KGST Acts, the time for filing
a claim in liquidation is fixed by the Code and the Regulations, not by the
CGST/KGST Acts. The kind of GST proceeding does not change the date on
which liquidation began or the last date for claims.
57.
We also observe that the Appellant rightly used Section 42 of the Code and
came to the Adjudicating Authority within fourteen days of the rejection. It argued
that even if the Liquidator cannot condone delay, the Adjudicating Authority can

[PAGE 23]
Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) No. 1752 of 2026

Page 23 of 25

do so under Section 60(5) read with Section 42 of the Code. Even if we assume
that the power exists, it can be used only for sufficient cause and where the time-
bound working of the liquidation is not harmed. The Code’s own limits are short.
A claim must be filed in thirty days and a challenge to the liquidator’s decision in
fourteen. A claimant who comes 896 days late is asking for a large exception to a
scheme built on short periods. It must show strong reasons for that.
58.
The Appellant’s explanation is that the liability crystallised on 21.02.2026
and that the claim was filed soon after. At most this explains the period after
21.02.2026. The claim was filed 29 days after the rectification application was
rejected on 24.03.2026. It does not explain the period from 08.11.2023 to
21.02.2026, which is more than two years and three months. For that period there
is no explanation at all. And, as we have seen, the date of crystallisation does not
decide when a claim must be filed under the Code.
59.
The Appellant does not say that it did not know about the liquidation. A
public announcement was made on 11.10.2023. By 2025 the Appellant had filed
IA No. 680(AHM)/2025 in the same company petition. In October and November
2025, the Liquidator wrote to it during the Section 74 proceedings of the
CGST/KGST Acts and told it about the liquidation. Even then it filed its claim
only on 22.04.2026. Waiting for the adjudication to finish was the Appellant’s
own choice. The Code did not require it, because a disputed or unquantified claim
can be filed.

60.
We are aware that the Appellant is a statutory authority, but this fact does
not give it a longer period. It has its own records of registered persons and has
access to public notices. ABG Shipyard (supra) itself stipulates such authorities
to follow the time periods in the Code strictly. Government dues find their place
in Section 53 of the Code, but only through the claims process.
61.
We need to appreciate that the Corporate Debtor has been in liquidation for
three years. The process has run on the claims received by 08.11.2023. If a claim
is admitted after this long, the Liquidator must reopen verification and revise the
list of stakeholders, and distribution may be affected. That is the harm the time
limits are meant to prevent. The amount is small, but the test is not the amount.
If this claim is admitted, other late claimants will ask for the same. We feel that it
is not the intention of the code. We find that no sufficient cause is shown for the
delay of 896 days. We also find that admitting the claim would be against the
time-bound working of the Code.
62.
We also keep in mind the position of the other stakeholders. Their claims
were filed and verified on the basis of the list prepared after 08.11.2023. They
have organised their expectations around that list. A statutory creditor stands in
the same line as other operational creditors, and Section 53 of the Code does not
put it ahead of the rest merely because it is the State. Letting it in three years late,
on a liability that did not exist when the list was made, would treat it better than
every creditor who obeyed the timelines.

63.
In view of the above discussion, we are of the considered view that the
Adjudicating Authority was right to dismiss the application. The Liquidator’s
rejection dated 02.05.2026 cannot be faulted. The claim was filed 896 days after
the last date. The Liquidator had no power to accept it. No sufficient cause is
shown for the Adjudicating Authority to condone the delay. And a claim founded
on a liability fixed after the liquidation commencement date does not fit the
scheme of Regulations 12 and 16.
64.
We do not find merit in the Appeal. The appeal is dismissed. There shall be
no order as to costs. All pending interlocutory applications, if any, stand closed.
