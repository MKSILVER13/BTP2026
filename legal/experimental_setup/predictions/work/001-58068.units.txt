u230 p121 ¶26 [27167-27275) | The applicant complained of a violation of Article 7 of the Convention (art. 7), which is worded as follows:
u231 p121  [27276-27279) Q | "1.

u234 p122 ¶2 [27604-27840) | This Article (art. 7) shall not prejudice the trial and punishment of any person for any act or omission which, at the time when it was committed, was criminal according to the general principles of law recognised by civilised nations."
u235 p122  [27841-28010) | He maintained that the definition of medicinal product contained in Article L. 511 of the Public Health Code was very imprecise and left a wide discretion to the courts.
u236 p122  [28011-28247) | The Court of Cassation’s case-law in this field was marked by arbitrariness and a lack of certainty which were themselves directly responsible for the conflicting classifications given to parapharmaceutical products by the lower courts.
u237 p122  [28248-28721) | This state of affairs still persisted and concerned all the substances in question, whether hydrogen peroxide, 70% strength alcohol or vitamin C. The case-law of the Court of Justice of the European Communities was not particularly helpful because it left it to the national courts to decide on a case-by-case basis whether a substance should be classified as a medicinal product and referred to notions that themselves lacked precision and were not sufficiently technical.
u238 p122  [28722-28853) | In short, the definition found in the legislation and the case-law failed to afford the requisite foreseeability and accessibility.

u242 p124 ¶28 [29420-29656) | Referring to the case-law of the Court, the Government argued that a law could be formulated in relatively general terms making it possible for its provisions to be adapted, through the process of interpretation, to changing situations.

u253 p125 ¶29 [31301-31612) | As the Court has already held, Article 7 (art. 7) embodies, inter alia, the principle that only the law can define a crime and prescribe a penalty (nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege) and the principle that the criminal law must not be extensively construed to an accused’s detriment, for instance by analogy.
u254 p125  [31613-31697) | From these principles it follows that an offence must be clearly defined in the law.
u255 p125  [31698-31940) | This requirement is satisfied where the individual can know from the wording of the relevant provision (art. 7) and, if need be, with the assistance of the courts’ interpretation of it, what acts and omissions will make him criminally liable.
u256 p125  [31941-32433) | When speaking of "law" Article 7 (art. 7) alludes to the very same concept as that to which the Convention refers elsewhere when using that term, a concept which comprises statutory law as well as case-law and implies qualitative requirements, notably those of accessibility and foreseeability (see, as the most recent authority, the S.W. and C.R. v. the United Kingdom judgments of 22 November 1995, Series A nos. 335-B and 335-C, pp. 41-42, para. 35, and pp. 68-69, para. 33, respectively).
u257 p125  [32434-32488) | In the present case only that last aspect is in issue.

u259 p127 ¶31 [32742-32926) | As the Court has already had occasion to note, it is a logical consequence of the principle that laws must be of general application that the wording of statutes is not always precise.
